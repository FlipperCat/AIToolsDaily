## Tana Review (2026): The AI-Native Notes App for People Who Outgrew Notion

Every notes app now has an AI button. Tana is one of the few that was built around the assumption that AI would be doing part of the organising, and after several months of using it as my main workspace, that design choice is both its biggest strength and the source of most of its rough edges.

This review covers what Tana actually is, where the AI features earn their keep, what it costs, and who should stay with something more conventional.

## What it is

Tana is an outliner. Every line you write is a node, nodes nest inside other nodes, and any node can be referenced from anywhere else. If you have used Roam Research, Logseq, or Workflowy, the writing surface will feel familiar.

What makes Tana different is the supertag. Adding a tag like `#meeting` or `#person` to a node does more than label it. It attaches a set of fields (date, attendees, status, whatever you defined), inherits any fields from parent tags, and makes that node show up in every search and view built around the tag. A note becomes a database record without you leaving the outline or opening a database.

Live searches then act as views over those records. A search for "all `#task` nodes with status not done and due this week" is a saved node you can drop anywhere, including inside today's daily note. That is the mechanism that lets Tana replace a task manager, a CRM, and a meeting log without building three separate systems.

Everything runs through a web app, with desktop wrappers and a mobile app that is mostly for capture rather than full editing.

## Key features

**Supertags with inheritance.** A `#client-meeting` tag can extend `#meeting`, picking up its fields and adding its own. This is the feature that makes structure grow with your notes rather than being designed up front.

**AI that acts on structure.** Because a node has typed fields, Tana's AI can fill them. Tag a pasted email as `#lead`, and it will pull out the company, contact, and next step into the right fields. Ask it to summarise the children of a node, and the result lands as a new child. The AI is exposed as commands you attach to tags or run from a menu, so it becomes part of a workflow rather than a chat window off to the side.

**Capture.** The mobile app records voice memos, transcribes them, and lets you route the result into a supertag with fields already extracted. Talking into your phone for thirty seconds and having a structured `#idea` node with a summary and a tag waiting on the desktop is the most delightful part of the product.

**Meeting notes.** Tana can join or record calls, transcribe them, and produce a structured note with a summary, decisions, and action items as tagged `#task` nodes. This overlaps with dedicated meeting tools; our [Granola review](/granola-ai-meeting-notes-review-2026/) covers the specialist option, and Granola's transcripts are better. Tana's advantage is that the action items are real tasks in the same system where the rest of your work lives.

**Calendar integration.** Events show in the daily note, and creating a meeting node from an event carries the attendees in as `#person` references. Small feature, large payoff for anyone whose day is meetings.

**Views.** Any set of nodes can be shown as a list, table, cards, or calendar. Tables with editable fields make Tana usable for light project tracking without a separate tool.

## Where it shines

Tana is at its best when you do not know what structure you need yet. You take notes for a few weeks, notice you keep writing the same kind of thing, add a supertag, and the structure is retroactively there. In Notion you would have had to decide on the database schema on day one and migrate pages into it.

The AI features also feel less bolted on than elsewhere. In most apps AI summarises a page and you copy the result somewhere. In Tana the summary is a node, the extracted entities are field values, and the tasks it found are already in your task view. That difference matters more than any individual feature.

For people who live in meetings and want one place where people, projects, and decisions link to each other, it is the most coherent system I have used.

## Cons and limitations

**The learning curve is real.** The concepts are simple once understood, but the app does little to teach them. New users hit a blank outline and a lot of unfamiliar terminology. Budget a week before judging it.

**Portability is weak.** Export produces a structured dump, not readable files. Compared with Obsidian's plain Markdown folder, this is the biggest reason to hesitate, and I would not move a decade of notes in without a plan for getting them out. Our [Notion vs Obsidian comparison](/compare/notion-vs-obsidian-2026/) is a useful framing: Tana sits closer to Notion on ownership and lock-in.

**Web-first, not offline-first.** The desktop app is a wrapper around the web app. It works without a connection for short stretches but this is not a tool for long flights.

**AI features draw down credits.** Transcription, summarisation, and field extraction consume a plan allowance. Heavy meeting use can exhaust it before the month ends, and the upgrade path is more money.

**Performance at scale.** Large workspaces with many live searches can lag on load. It has improved over the year but it is still noticeable on older machines.

**Team features lag.** Sharing and permissions exist but are coarse next to Notion. Tana is a strong personal system and an acceptable small-team one.

**Mobile is capture, not editing.** Rearranging an outline on a phone is fiddly. Plan to do real work on a desktop.

## Pricing

Tana has a free tier that covers the core outliner and supertags with limits on AI usage and workspace size. Paid plans add more AI credits, meeting transcription minutes, and larger workspaces. As of September 2026, the entry paid plan is priced in roughly the same band as Notion's and Mem's paid tiers, in the mid-teens of dollars per month on annual billing, with higher tiers scaling AI allowances and team features.

Prices and credit allowances have changed more than once over the past year, so check the current pricing page rather than relying on any figure here. The practical question is not the monthly fee but how quickly your meeting and AI use consumes the included credits.

## Who it's for

Tana fits people whose work is relational: consultants, founders, researchers, and managers who need people, projects, meetings, and tasks to reference each other. It fits people who enjoy building systems and are willing to invest a week to learn one. And it fits anyone frustrated that their notes app and task manager and CRM are three products that do not talk to each other.

It does not fit people who want a fast, low-friction place to write. It does not fit teams that need polished shared documentation. And it is a poor choice for anyone whose first requirement is owning their data as plain files.

If you want the AI-assisted knowledge management without the structural commitment, [Mem](/mem-ai-personal-knowledge-review-2026/) is the lower-effort option: it organises for you rather than giving you the tools to organise. If you want the polished pages and team features, [Notion](/reviews/notion-review-2026/) remains the safer default.

## Verdict

Tana is the most interesting notes app I have used in years, and I say that as someone who has cycled through most of them. The supertag model is a genuine idea, not a feature, and the AI integration follows from it rather than being glued on top.

It is also demanding. It asks you to learn a new mental model, it keeps your data in a format that is hard to leave, and its AI features cost real money once you rely on them. Those are not small caveats.

My recommendation is to try the free tier for two weeks with one real workflow, meetings or a project, and see whether the structure starts to appear on its own. If it does, Tana will probably become the centre of your work. If after two weeks it still feels like a puzzle, that is a fair verdict too, and a simpler tool will serve you better.
