Most AI writing tools in early 2023 have the same shape: a sidebar of templates, a tone selector, a credit counter, and a big **Generate** button. **Lex** goes the other way. It's a clean, Google Docs–style editor with AI tucked quietly underneath, and it only speaks when you ask it to. After a few weeks of drafting in it, that restraint turns out to be the whole point.

## What Lex is

Lex is a browser-based writing app built by the team behind the Every newsletter, with GPT-3 wired into the editor. There is no dashboard, no template gallery, and no marketing-copy wizard. You open a document, you see a blinking cursor on a white page, and you write.

The AI shows up in three places:

- **Inline continuation.** Type `+++` on a new line and hit enter, and Lex drafts the next few sentences based on everything above it.
- **Feedback.** Ask Lex to critique the draft and it responds like an editor who read the whole thing, not a spell-checker reacting to one sentence.
- **Titles.** It suggests headlines from the body copy, which is genuinely useful when you've been staring at a piece too long to name it.

Access is currently waitlisted. It's free while in beta, and it runs entirely in the browser with real-time collaboration and comments, so sharing a draft works about how you'd expect from a modern doc tool.

## Key features

**The `+++` continuation.** This is the feature everything else hangs off. Because Lex reads your actual document as context, the continuation matches what you've already written far better than pasting three paragraphs into a chat window does. In practice I use it to break a stall: I write until I run dry, drop a `+++`, read the two or three sentences it offers, delete most of them, and keep the one idea that got me moving again. Used that way it's excellent. Used as an autopilot it produces exactly the smooth, agreeable, forgettable prose you'd expect from GPT-3.

**AI feedback on the draft.** You can ask questions about your own writing — where it drags, what's unclear, what a skeptical reader would push back on. The answers are inconsistent but occasionally sharp, and they arrive fast enough that it's worth asking before you send a draft to a human. It won't replace an editor. It will catch the paragraph you knew was weak and were hoping nobody would notice.

**Headline suggestions.** Small feature, disproportionate value. Lex reads the finished piece and proposes titles. Roughly one in five is usable as-is, and the rest at least show you which angle the text is actually arguing.

**A genuinely good editor.** Easy to undersell this. Typography is clean, the page is uncluttered, keyboard shortcuts work, autosave is invisible, and comments and sharing behave like a normal collaborative document. A lot of AI writing tools are impressive models bolted onto a bad text box. Lex is the reverse, and for people who write for a living that trade is the right one.

## Pros

- **No mode-switching.** Drafting, getting unstuck, and getting feedback all happen in the same window. Compared to keeping [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review/) open in a second tab and shuttling paragraphs back and forth, the friction drop is real.
- **Restraint.** The AI never volunteers. Nothing autocompletes while you're mid-sentence, nothing nags you about tone. For anyone who finds aggressive suggestion engines distracting, this is the selling point.
- **Whole-document context.** Continuations and feedback are informed by the full draft, not a pasted excerpt, and it shows in how well the voice holds.
- **Free right now.** During the beta there's no card required and no credit meter counting down.
- **Fast and quiet.** It loads instantly and stays out of the way — closer to writing in a plain editor than using a "platform."

## Cons and limitations

- **Waitlist access.** You can't just sign up and start today, which makes it a hard tool to recommend to someone with a deadline this week.
- **GPT-3, not the newest model.** The prose is competent and a little flat. It repeats structures, reaches for stock transitions, and rarely surprises you. It's a nudge, not a collaborator.
- **No pricing clarity.** Free beta is pleasant until it isn't. If you build a workflow on Lex now, you're accepting that the terms will change.
- **Thin on editing tools.** There's no grammar engine, readability scoring, or style guide enforcement. If that's what you need, [Grammarly](/reviews/grammarly-review/) or a [Hemingway-style readability checker](/compare/grammarly-vs-hemingway/) still has to sit downstream.
- **Not built for volume or SEO.** No bulk generation, no keyword briefs, no CMS export pipeline. Content teams shipping thirty posts a month will find it underpowered — that's [Jasper's](/compare/jasper-vs-chatgpt-2023/) territory.
- **No offline mode or mobile app.** Browser only.
- **Paraphrasing and rewriting are basic.** For heavy rephrasing work a dedicated tool like [QuillBot](/reviews/quillbot-review-2023/) is still more direct.

## Pricing

As of February 2023, Lex is **free during its beta**, with access granted from a waitlist. There's no published paid tier yet and no credit limit that I've hit in normal daily use. The team has been open that this is a beta pricing decision rather than a business model, so assume paid plans are coming and budget accordingly. Prices and terms for anything in beta change quickly — check the site before you commit a team to it.

## Who it's for

**A good fit if you:**

- Write essays, newsletters, or long-form posts and want AI available without leaving your draft.
- Get blocked mid-piece more often than you get blocked at the blank page.
- Find suggestion-happy tools distracting and want the AI to stay silent by default.
- Value editor quality — typography, focus, collaboration — as much as model quality.

**Look elsewhere if you:**

- Need to produce high volumes of SEO or marketing copy on a schedule.
- Want grammar correction, plagiarism checks, or brand-voice enforcement built in.
- Need access today and can't wait on a list.
- Want to choose your model or tune parameters.

## Verdict

Lex is the most tasteful AI writing tool I've used so far, and taste is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The underlying model isn't better than what you get free in a chat window — it's the same generation of tech, and the prose it produces is just as average. What's different is the packaging: a serious writing environment where AI is a tool on the desk rather than the reason the desk exists.

That makes it narrow. It won't help a content team hit a quota and it won't fix your grammar. But if you're a writer who wants a clean page, whole-document context, and a nudge available on the exact keystroke you need it, Lex earns the tab. Just don't build anything load-bearing on the free beta — get on the waitlist, draft a few real pieces in it, and decide again when the pricing lands.
