Citation counts are a blunt instrument. A paper cited 500 times might be a landmark study, or it might be a famous result that 200 of those papers were written to refute. Traditional databases can't tell you which.

**Scite** was built to fix that. It indexes the sentences where papers cite each other and uses machine learning to classify each citation as supporting, contrasting, or just mentioning the work. The result is a quick read on how a paper has actually been received, not just how often it's been named.

With ChatGPT dominating conversations about AI and writing this year, and general chatbots notorious for making up references, a tool grounded in real citation data is worth a close look. I spent about two weeks using scite for a literature scan on a health-science topic and for fact-checking claims in articles.

## What scite is

Scite is a research platform centered on "Smart Citations." For any paper in its index, it shows:

- How many times the paper has been cited
- How many of those citations **support** its findings
- How many **contrast** or dispute them
- How many simply **mention** it
- The actual sentence from each citing paper, with a link to the source

It's aimed at researchers, students, librarians, and anyone who needs to judge the weight of evidence behind a claim, including journalists and science writers.

## Key features

### Smart Citations

This is the core product. Open a paper's report and you get a list of citation statements you can filter by type, by section of the citing paper (introduction, methods, results, discussion), and by year. Seeing the contrasting citations first is the fastest way I've found to learn where a field disagrees.

### Search across citation statements

You can search the text of citation statements themselves, not just titles and abstracts. Searching a specific claim often surfaces papers that tested it directly, which is hard to do with keyword search in a regular database.

### Dashboards and alerts

You can build dashboards for a set of papers (your own publications, a journal, or a reading list) and get alerts when new citations appear, including contrasting ones. For authors, this is a useful way to track how their work is being received.

### Reference Check

Upload a manuscript and scite checks its reference list, flagging cited papers that have been retracted or that have notable contrasting evidence. It's a practical pre-submission sanity check.

### Browser extension and integrations

The browser extension adds scite badges to papers on publisher sites and some search engines, so you see support and contrast counts while browsing. There are also plugins for reference managers such as Zotero.

### Assistant (early)

Scite has been adding an AI assistant that answers questions using its citation index and shows the references it drew from. It's early, and I'd treat it as a starting point for searching rather than a source of answers, but grounding responses in real papers addresses the biggest weakness of general chatbots for research.

## Pros

- **Adds context raw counts can't.** Knowing a paper has a pile of contrasting citations changes how you read it.
- **Every claim links to a source.** You can click through to the citing sentence and the full paper.
- **Speeds up literature scanning.** Filtering to contrasting citations in the discussion sections is a quick way to find the debates.
- **Reference Check catches retractions.** Useful before submitting or publishing.
- **Good fit with existing tools.** The extension and reference-manager plugins mean you don't have to change how you work.

## Cons and limitations

- **Classification errors.** Automated labeling of citation intent is hard. I found citations marked as "supporting" that were closer to neutral, and hedged language sometimes gets misread. Always read the snippet.
- **Most citations are "mentioning."** For many papers, the large majority of citations are neutral mentions, so the supporting/contrasting signal can be thin.
- **Coverage gaps.** Scite depends on access to full text. Coverage is strong in biomedical and life sciences and patchier in some humanities and social-science areas, and very recent papers may have few indexed citations yet.
- **Snippets lack full context.** A single sentence can misrepresent a nuanced argument.
- **Paywall for serious use.** The free experience is limited, and the individual subscription is a real cost for students without institutional access.
- **Learning curve.** The interface is dense. It takes a session or two to learn which filters actually matter.

## Pricing

As of March 2023, scite offers a free trial and limited free features, with a paid individual plan in the range of roughly $20 per month and a discount for annual billing. Institutional and enterprise licenses are sold separately, and many universities already subscribe. Prices change often in this category, so check scite's site for current rates, and ask your library about institutional access first.

## Who scite is for

**Good fit:**
- Graduate students and researchers doing literature reviews
- Academic authors who want to track how their work is cited
- Librarians supporting research teams
- Science journalists and fact-checkers who need to judge whether a finding has held up
- Anyone in evidence-heavy fields like medicine and public health

**Less useful for:**
- Casual users who want quick summaries of a topic
- Fields where scite's full-text coverage is thin
- People looking for a writing assistant (that's not what this is)

## How it compares to general AI chatbots

The contrast with ChatGPT is worth spelling out. Ask a general chatbot for sources on a topic and you may get plausible-looking citations that don't exist. Scite works in the opposite direction: it starts from real citation statements and lets you explore them. The two can complement each other. Use a chatbot to brainstorm search terms and outline, then use scite to see what the literature actually says.

## Tips for getting the most out of scite

1. **Start with contrasting citations.** They show you the debates fastest.
2. **Filter by section.** Citations in the results and discussion sections tend to be more substantive than those in introductions.
3. **Click through before you quote.** Never cite a paper based only on a snippet.
4. **Run Reference Check before submitting.** It takes minutes and can save an embarrassing retraction citation.
5. **Set alerts on key papers.** You'll hear about new challenges to findings you rely on.

## Verdict

Scite does something no traditional database does well: it shows you how research has been received, with the evidence one click away. The citation classifications aren't perfect, coverage varies by field, and the paid plan is a real expense, but for anyone who has to judge whether a finding holds up, it's a meaningful time-saver.

**Rating: 4 / 5.** Highly recommended for researchers and students with institutional access; worth a trial for independent researchers and science writers before committing to a subscription.
