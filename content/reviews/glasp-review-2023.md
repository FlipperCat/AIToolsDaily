Most people read on the web the same way: open twenty tabs, skim, forget almost all of it, and bookmark a few pages they never open again. Glasp is a browser extension built to fix the "forget almost all of it" part. You highlight text on any web page, Glasp saves it to a personal library, and, unusually, it makes that library social.

The tool has picked up a burst of attention this winter for a second, separate reason: a free companion extension that summarizes YouTube videos using ChatGPT. We used both for several weeks of research reading to see whether Glasp earns a permanent spot in the browser toolbar.

## What Glasp Is

Glasp is a free extension for Chromium browsers (Chrome, Edge, Brave and similar) that adds a highlighter to every page you read. Select text, pick a color, optionally add a note, and the highlight is saved to your Glasp profile along with the page title and link. The next time you visit that page, your highlights reappear in place.

The social layer is what separates it from older highlighters. Your profile shows what you've been reading and highlighting, you can follow other readers, and Glasp suggests people whose reading overlaps with yours. Think of it as a public commonplace book.

The AI angle is mostly in the companion extension, **YouTube Summary with ChatGPT**, which adds a transcript panel next to YouTube videos and a button that sends that transcript to ChatGPT for a summary.

## Key Features

### Web highlighting with notes and tags

Highlighting is fast and unobtrusive. You get several colors, a note field per highlight, and tags at the page level. A sidebar lists every highlight on the current page so you can jump between them. It handles most article layouts well; heavily scripted web apps and some paywalled readers are less reliable.

### Kindle import

Glasp can pull in your Kindle highlights, which puts book notes and web notes in one searchable place. For anyone who reads seriously on both, this alone justifies installing it.

### Export to your notes app

Highlights can be copied as Markdown or sent to tools like Notion and Obsidian. That matters, because a highlighter is only as useful as where the highlights end up. If you already keep a second brain in Obsidian, Glasp becomes the capture layer rather than yet another silo.

### Social discovery

You can browse other users' profiles, see popular highlights on a given article, and follow people with similar reading habits. In practice, this works best in niches with active users: startups, product management, productivity, and AI. Outside those, the feed gets thin.

### YouTube Summary with ChatGPT

Install the companion extension and YouTube videos get a transcript box beside the player. Click the ChatGPT button and it opens ChatGPT with the transcript and a summary prompt already loaded. For a 40-minute talk, you get a bullet-point summary in under a minute, and you can follow up in ChatGPT with questions like "what were the three concrete recommendations?"

It's a simple trick, but a useful one. If you're new to prompting ChatGPT directly, our [getting started with ChatGPT guide](/getting-started-with-chatgpt-2023/) covers how to push follow-up questions further.

## How It Performs in Practice

We used Glasp across roughly a hundred articles and a dozen long YouTube talks.

**Highlighting** was reliable on standard blogs, news sites, and documentation. It occasionally failed to restore highlights after a site changed its layout, which is a known weakness of every web highlighter that anchors to page text.

**Retrieval** is where Glasp is decent but not great. Search works across your highlights, but there's no smart resurfacing of old notes and no way to ask questions across your whole library. The AI is not yet applied to the highlights themselves.

**YouTube summaries** were genuinely useful for talks, interviews, and tutorials with clean auto-captions. They were weaker on videos with music, multiple speakers talking over each other, or poor auto-generated captions. Long videos can also exceed what ChatGPT handles in one message, so summaries of very long content sometimes skip the second half. Treat the summary as a map of the video, not a replacement for it.

## Pros

- **Free and quick to set up.** Install, sign in, start highlighting.
- **Web and Kindle in one library.** Few free tools combine both cleanly.
- **Good exports.** Markdown, Notion, and Obsidian support keep your notes portable.
- **YouTube summaries save real time** on talks and interviews, especially when deciding whether a video is worth watching in full.
- **Discovery is fun** in the niches where the community is active.

## Cons and Limitations

- **Privacy needs attention.** A social-first highlighter is the wrong default for confidential work reading. Check visibility settings before you start.
- **The AI layer is thin.** The summary feature is a bridge to ChatGPT, not Glasp's own model. If ChatGPT is at capacity, which still happens at peak times, the feature stalls.
- **Transcript quality caps summary quality.** Bad captions in, bad summary out.
- **Highlights can break** when pages change their structure.
- **Limited mobile story.** It's a desktop browser tool first. Reading on your phone means your highlights don't follow you the same way.
- **No questions across your library.** You can't yet ask "what have I read about pricing strategy?" and get an answer drawn from your highlights.

## Pricing

As of February 2023, Glasp is free, including the highlighter, Kindle import, exports, and the YouTube summary extension. There's no paid plan at the moment. The YouTube summary feature works with a free ChatGPT account, though ChatGPT's new paid Plus plan helps if you hit availability limits during busy hours.

Free tools with growing user bases often add premium tiers later, so don't build a critical workflow that assumes everything stays free forever. Keeping regular Markdown exports is a cheap insurance policy.

## How It Compares

Glasp competes with a crowded field of highlighters and read-later apps. Paid tools like Readwise focus on resurfacing your highlights over time; Glasp focuses on capturing and sharing them. Diigo and older highlighters offer more annotation depth but feel dated and lack the YouTube trick.

For long-form writing that builds on your notes, pair Glasp with a dedicated writing tool. We liked [Lex](/reviews/lex-ai-writing-editor-review-2023/) for turning research notes into drafts. And if you're researching with chatbots rather than articles, our [ChatGPT vs YouChat comparison](/compare/chatgpt-vs-youchat-2023/) looks at which assistant handles research questions better.

## Who It's For

- **Students and researchers** who read a lot on the web and want highlights in one place.
- **Obsidian and Notion users** who want a clean capture tool feeding their notes.
- **Kindle readers** who want book highlights alongside web clippings.
- **Anyone who watches long YouTube talks** and wants a quick summary before committing an hour.
- **Public learners** who like sharing what they read. If "learning in public" appeals to you, the social profile is a feature, not a risk.

It's a weaker fit for anyone reading confidential material, mobile-first readers, and people who want AI to organize and query their notes for them.

## Verdict

Glasp is a solid free highlighter with two standout touches: Kindle import and the YouTube-to-ChatGPT summary extension. The social layer is interesting but optional, and it comes with a privacy trade-off you need to manage. The AI is a thin bridge to ChatGPT rather than a deep feature, so don't install Glasp expecting an AI research assistant.

Install it if you read heavily on the web and want those highlights to land somewhere useful. Skip it, or lock down the privacy settings first, if most of your reading is work material you wouldn't want on a public profile.

**Rating: 3.8/5**. Free, practical, and genuinely useful for summaries, with privacy defaults and shallow AI holding it back.
