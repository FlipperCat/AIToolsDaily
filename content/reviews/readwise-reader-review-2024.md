Most read-it-later apps end up as a pile of articles you'll never open. Readwise Reader wants to be where you actually read them and keep what matters. It collects articles, PDFs, newsletters, RSS feeds, and YouTube videos in one place, adds serious highlighting and an AI assistant called Ghostreader, and pipes everything into your notes app.

I've used Reader as my main reading app for several months, alongside ChatGPT and Claude for research. Here's what works, what doesn't, and who should pay for it.

## What Readwise Reader is

Readwise started as a service that collects your Kindle and web highlights and resurfaces them through a daily spaced-repetition review. Reader is the company's own reading app, built so highlights never have to leave that system.

You save content from a browser extension, the mobile share sheet, a forwarding email address, or by subscribing to feeds. Reader keeps two main spaces:

- **Library:** things you chose to save, sorted into Inbox, Later, Shortlist, and Archive
- **Feed:** everything arriving automatically from RSS and newsletter subscriptions, kept separate so it doesn't bury your deliberate saves

Anything you highlight syncs to Readwise, and from there to your notes tool.

## Key features

### One inbox for every format

Reader handles more formats than any read-it-later app I've tested:

- Web articles, with good parsing on most sites
- PDFs, with highlights that stay attached to the page
- EPUB books you upload yourself (DRM-free files)
- Email newsletters, through a dedicated Reader address
- RSS feeds, including an OPML import
- YouTube videos, with a synced transcript you can highlight like text
- Twitter/X threads, saved as a readable document

Newsletters are the big one for me. Moving subscriptions out of Gmail and into Reader made my inbox noticeably calmer.

### Ghostreader, the AI assistant

Ghostreader is built into the reading view. You can select a word or passage and ask it to define, explain, or simplify it. At the document level, you can generate a summary, ask questions about the text, or produce discussion questions to test your understanding. Reader can also create a short summary automatically when you save something, which helps when you're deciding what to read.

The most interesting part is **custom prompts**. You can edit the built-in prompts or write your own, using template variables for the document text, your highlight, or the title. I have one that turns a highlighted passage into a flashcard-style question and one that extracts every claim with a number attached. If you want more control over the model, you can connect your own OpenAI API key.

Ghostreader works best on focused tasks tied to a passage. Its document summaries are serviceable, not deep. They catch the main argument but flatten nuance, much like the summarizers we covered in our [guide to summarizing documents with AI](/tutorials/summarize-documents-with-ai/).

### Highlighting that goes somewhere

Highlighting is quick on desktop and mobile, with keyboard shortcuts for highlighting, tagging, and adding notes. Everything flows into Readwise, which exports to Obsidian, Notion, Logseq, Evernote, Roam, and others. Once it's set up, a highlight becomes a note in your knowledge base without any extra work.

### Text-to-speech

Reader can read documents aloud with natural-sounding voices, including in the mobile apps, with speed control. It's not as polished as a dedicated listening app like [Speechify](/reviews/speechify-review-2023/), but it's included and works well enough for articles during a commute. If you'd rather hear a conversation-style summary of your sources, that's a different job. See our [NotebookLM Audio Overviews guide](/tutorials/notebooklm-audio-overviews-guide-2024/).

### Filtered views and search

Power users can build saved views using a query language, such as "unread PDFs tagged research" or "newsletters from the last seven days." Full-text search covers your whole library.

## Pros

- **Handles almost every reading format** in one app, which is rare
- **Excellent highlighting** with a real export pipeline to notes tools
- **Ghostreader custom prompts** make the AI adaptable instead of a gimmick
- **Separating Feed from Library** keeps subscriptions from burying deliberate saves
- **Strong keyboard support** for fast triage on desktop
- **Active development**, with frequent, visible updates

## Cons and limitations

- **Price.** Pocket and Instapaper cover basic saving for free. Reader only pays off if you use the highlighting and export.
- **Learning curve.** Feed, Library, triage states, views, and tags are a lot for someone who just wants to read.
- **It can become another inbox.** Easy RSS and newsletter capture makes it tempting to subscribe to everything. Without discipline, the Feed turns into another pile of guilt.
- **AI depth is limited.** Ghostreader answers questions about one document at a time. It doesn't reason across your whole library the way a dedicated research tool might.
- **Parsing isn't perfect.** Paywalled sites, heavy JavaScript pages, and complex PDF layouts sometimes come through messy. Scanned PDFs are especially hit or miss.
- **Some rough edges.** Features ship quickly, and a few feel unfinished across platforms.

## Pricing (as of October 2024)

Reader isn't sold separately. It's included in the **full Readwise subscription**, which costs roughly **$10 per month billed annually**, with a higher month-to-month price. A cheaper Readwise Lite plan covers highlight syncing but doesn't include Reader. There's a free trial, and Readwise has offered student and regional discounts in the past. Check the current pricing page, since plans change.

Ghostreader is included. If you connect your own OpenAI key, usage on that key is billed by OpenAI separately.

## How it compares

- **vs Pocket and Instapaper:** Simpler and cheaper, but much weaker on PDFs, newsletters, and getting highlights out.
- **vs Matter:** A closer competitor with a nice reading experience and audio, but Reader handles more formats and has a stronger export system.
- **vs chat-with-your-PDF tools:** Tools like [ChatPDF](/reviews/chatpdf-review-2023/) are better for interrogating one long document. Reader is better as the place your reading lives day to day.

## Who it's for

Reader fits:

- **Researchers, writers, and students** who read a lot and need their highlights in a notes system
- **Newsletter-heavy readers** who want subscriptions out of their email
- **Obsidian, Notion, and Logseq users** building a personal knowledge base
- **Anyone already paying for Readwise**, since Reader is included

It's probably not worth it if you save a few articles a week and rarely highlight anything. A free app will do.

## Verdict

Readwise Reader is the best read-it-later app for people who read to learn and want to keep what they read. Broad format support, strong highlighting, and the export pipeline make it more than a bookmark list. Ghostreader is useful because it's customizable and stays close to the text, even if its summaries won't replace reading closely.

It costs more than the alternatives and takes some setup. If your reading ends in notes, essays, or decisions, it's worth trying. If you just want to park articles for later, it's more than you need.

**Rating: 4.4/5**
