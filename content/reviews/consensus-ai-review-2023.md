Ask ChatGPT a research question and it'll answer confidently — sometimes with citations that don't exist. That gap, between a fluent answer and a *sourced* one, is exactly what **Consensus** was built to close. It's an AI search engine that only draws from published scientific research, and in 2023 it's become one of the more genuinely useful tools for anyone who needs evidence rather than vibes.

## What it is

Consensus is a search engine that sits on top of a large database of peer-reviewed papers — over 200 million, spanning medicine, economics, psychology, social science, and more. You type a natural-language question, and instead of a list of blue links, it reads across the relevant studies and hands you a synthesized answer with each claim tied to a specific paper.

The pitch is simple: get to the research-backed answer without spending an afternoon in a database. It's aimed at researchers, students, clinicians, analysts, and curious professionals who want to know "what does the evidence actually say?" and want the receipts attached.

## Key features

**Direct question answering.** Ask "Does intermittent fasting improve blood pressure?" and Consensus returns a plain-language summary of what studies have found, with citations inline. It's built for yes/no and effect-oriented questions, which is where it feels most natural.

**The Consensus Meter.** For questions where studies might disagree, this is the standout feature. The Meter aggregates the findings of the top papers and shows, at a glance, how much of the literature says yes, no, or "it's mixed." It's a fast way to sense whether a claim is settled science or still contested — something a single chatbot answer can't convey.

**GPT-4 powered summaries.** Consensus layers large language models on top of its retrieval, using them to summarize individual papers and, in its Copilot feature, to synthesize across sources. Crucially, the summaries are grounded in the actual papers it retrieves, which keeps the fabricated-citation problem far smaller than it is with a general-purpose chatbot.

**Study details at a glance.** Each result shows the study type, sample size, and publication, so you can quickly weigh a large randomized trial against a small observational study without opening every PDF.

## Pros

- Answers are grounded in real, citable peer-reviewed papers
- The Consensus Meter gives instant signal on where the evidence stands
- Much faster than manually searching a database for a first pass
- Clean, genuinely easy-to-use interface — no query syntax to learn
- A usable free tier that covers casual and light research needs

## Cons and limitations

- **Best for empirical yes/no questions.** It's less useful for open-ended, theoretical, or purely qualitative topics where "the evidence" isn't a tidy stack of findings.
- **Summaries can flatten nuance.** A one-line takeaway may drop the caveats, sample limits, or context that make a study meaningful. You still need to read the source.
- **Coverage skews to certain fields.** Health, medicine, and social science are well represented; niche or very new subfields can be thin.
- **Not a full literature-review tool.** It's excellent for orientation and quick answers, but organizing, screening, and extracting data across dozens of papers is a different job — closer to what a tool like [Elicit](/reviews/elicit-ai-review-2023/) is built for.
- **AI features are capped on free.** Heavy users will hit the monthly limit and need Premium.

## Pricing (approximate, as of August 2023)

- **Free** — unlimited searches with a limited number of AI credits (summaries, Copilot, GPT-4 synthesis) per month.
- **Premium** — roughly $8.99/month billed annually (higher month-to-month), unlocking unlimited AI features, GPT-4 summaries, and advanced filters.
- **Teams/Enterprise** — custom pricing for institutions.

As always, treat these figures as a snapshot; research tools adjust plans frequently, so check the current pricing before subscribing.

## Who it's for

Consensus is a strong fit for **students, researchers, healthcare professionals, journalists, and evidence-minded generalists** who regularly ask factual questions that have a research answer. If you've ever pasted a health or science question into a chatbot and then worried whether the sources were real, this is the tool that fixes that worry.

It pairs well with other tools rather than replacing them. Use it alongside a general assistant like [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review/) for drafting, a cited web search like [Perplexity](/reviews/perplexity-review/) for broader queries, or a document Q&A tool like [ChatPDF](/reviews/chatpdf-review-2023/) when you already have the papers in hand.

A practical workflow that works well in 2023: start a topic in Consensus to see the shape of the evidence and the Consensus Meter reading, note the two or three most-cited papers it surfaces, then open those directly to read the methods and limitations yourself. That gives you the speed of AI synthesis for orientation without outsourcing your judgment — you decide which findings actually hold up. Treat Consensus as the fast first pass, not the final word, and it rarely steers you wrong.

## Verdict

Consensus does one thing and does it well: it turns "what does the research say?" into a fast, sourced answer. The Consensus Meter is the feature that sells it — being able to see at a glance whether the literature broadly agrees is genuinely valuable, and nothing else in the 2023 landscape delivers that as cleanly.

It won't write your paper or organize your entire review, and its summaries are a starting point, not a substitute for reading the studies. But as a way to get oriented on an evidence question in minutes instead of hours — while staying honest about your sources — Consensus has earned a spot in the research toolkit. For anyone who takes citations seriously, the free tier alone is worth trying.
