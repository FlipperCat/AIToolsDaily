Read AI sits in an odd spot in the meeting assistant market. Every competitor is racing to write better summaries; Read AI does that too, then adds a layer most rivals skip — scoring your meetings and the people in them. Engagement percentages. Sentiment readings. Talk-time breakdowns. Reports that tell you how much of your week went into calls that nobody was paying attention to.

Whether that layer is insight or theatre is the whole question. I ran Read AI across several weeks of real calls to find out.

## What is Read AI?

Read AI is a meeting assistant that joins your Zoom, Google Meet, or Microsoft Teams calls, records and transcribes them, then produces a summary with action items and decisions. That part is table stakes in 2025 and works about as well as you would expect.

The layer on top is what the product is actually selling. Read AI also generates:

- **Engagement and sentiment scores** for the meeting overall and per participant
- **Speaker analytics** — talk time, interruptions, monologue length, question ratio
- **Meeting reports** delivered by email after each call, with a scored highlight reel
- **Cross-surface summaries** that extend the same treatment to email threads and Slack or Teams messages
- **Search** across everything it has captured, so you can ask what was decided about a topic without remembering which call it was in

There is also a scheduling assistant that proposes times based on your calendar, though it is the weakest piece of the suite.

## Key features

### Transcription and summaries

Accuracy on clear audio is good. Native English speakers on decent headsets transcribe cleanly, speaker labels are mostly right, and the summaries pick up the actual decisions rather than restating the agenda. Accented speech, crosstalk, and people dialling in from a car all degrade it, which is true of every tool in this category.

Summaries land on the concise side. You get a short recap, a list of action items with owners where it can infer them, and key questions raised. Compared with the wall-of-bullets approach some competitors take, this is a real improvement — you can read the whole thing in twenty seconds.

### Engagement and sentiment scoring

Each meeting gets an engagement percentage, and each participant gets their own. The system infers this from camera state, talk time, speech patterns, and facial cues where video is on.

Used at the aggregate level, this is mildly useful. A recurring meeting that scores badly week after week is a recurring meeting that should probably be an email, and having a number to point at makes that argument easier to win. I did kill one standing call on this evidence.

Used at the individual level, it is a bad idea. The score does not distinguish an engaged listener from a disengaged one — it largely rewards talking and having your camera on. Anyone who processes quietly will look disengaged. Any team that ties this to performance review will get exactly the behaviour it measures. Read AI does not encourage that use, but the data is right there in the UI.

### Speaker analytics

More defensible than the sentiment work. Talk-time distribution, interruption counts, and question ratios are directly observable rather than inferred, and they surface real patterns — the manager who takes 70% of the airtime in a "team" meeting, the person who never gets a word in. As a private self-check it is genuinely useful.

### Beyond meetings

Read AI's expansion into email and messaging summaries is the most interesting strategic bet. Connect your inbox and Slack, and it will digest long threads and surface what needs a reply. The quality is decent on long, linear threads and mediocre on scattered ones. Whether you want one vendor reading your calls, your inbox, and your team chat is a question for your security review, not your productivity review.

### Search across history

Once you have a few months of meetings captured, the search is the feature that quietly becomes indispensable. Asking what was agreed about a launch date and getting the relevant moment from a call six weeks ago is the single best argument for using any tool in this category.

## Pros

- Clean, genuinely concise summaries — not bullet-point sprawl
- Solid transcription accuracy on good audio
- Speaker analytics expose real meeting dysfunction
- Cross-surface coverage (meetings, email, messaging) under one search
- Fast post-meeting delivery; the report is usually waiting before you have switched tabs
- Free tier is usable for light users, not just a demo

## Cons and limitations

- **The scoring invites misuse.** Per-person engagement metrics in a shared workspace are a management temptation this product does not need.
- **Sentiment analysis is soft.** It reads meeting mechanics, not minds. Do not act on it alone.
- **Visible bot in the participant list.** Some clients and candidates react badly to that, and it is an awkward conversation to have at the top of every external call.
- **Privacy surface is broad.** Granting calendar, meeting, email, and chat access to one vendor is a meaningful decision. Check your data retention and regional processing requirements first.
- **Scheduling assistant underdelivers.** A dedicated scheduling tool does this better.
- **Accuracy degrades on messy audio** — heavy accents, poor mics, and rapid crosstalk still cause trouble.
- **Per-seat pricing adds up** across a whole team, especially when only a few people read the analytics.

## Pricing

As of August 2025, Read AI runs a free tier with a limited number of meeting transcripts and summaries per month, which is enough to evaluate it properly and enough for someone with a light meeting load. Paid individual plans land in the rough range of $20 to $30 per user per month on annual billing, with monthly billing noticeably higher, and enterprise tiers above that adding admin controls, longer retention, and broader integrations.

Treat those figures as approximate — this category repackages its tiers frequently, and the free-tier meeting cap in particular has moved more than once. Check the current pricing page before committing a team.

The relevant comparison is not free versus paid but per-seat versus alternatives. [Otter](/reviews/16-otter-ai-review/) and [Fireflies](/reviews/17-fireflies-ai-review/) both compete at similar or lower per-seat prices for transcript-and-summary work, and the analytics layer is what you are paying the premium for. If nobody on your team will open the analytics, you are overpaying.

## Who it is for

**Good fit:** managers and team leads who genuinely want to audit meeting hygiene, revenue teams that care about talk-time ratios on customer calls, and anyone whose work is spread across meetings, email, and chat and who wants one searchable record of all three.

**Poor fit:** solo operators and small teams who just need reliable notes — cheaper, more focused tools do that job. Also a poor fit for anyone in a strict compliance environment, or for teams whose culture would curdle the moment engagement scores became visible.

**Depends:** consultants and agencies. The searchable multi-client history is valuable; the visible bot on client calls is a friction point you will have to manage.

## Verdict

Read AI is a good meeting assistant carrying a differentiator that is more double-edged than the marketing admits. The transcription and summaries are solid, the search across accumulated history is the real long-term payoff, and the speaker analytics can prompt genuinely useful conversations about how a team runs its calls.

The engagement and sentiment scoring is where I would push back. It is inferred from proxies, it is presented with more confidence than it has earned, and in the wrong organisation it becomes a surveillance metric rather than a diagnostic. Read AI is not responsible for how customers use it, but a feature that invites misuse this easily deserves a warning label.

If the analytics genuinely appeal to you, it is worth the premium and the free tier will tell you within a week. If you just want accurate, searchable notes, look at the more focused options first — our [Granola vs Fathom comparison](/compare/granola-vs-fathom-2025/) covers the lighter-weight end of the market, and [Fathom vs Otter](/compare/fathom-vs-otter-2024/) is the better starting point if price per seat is your main constraint.

**Rating: solid, with a caveat.** Buy it for the notes and the search. Be deliberate about who can see the scores.
