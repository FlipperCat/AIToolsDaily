## AIVA Review (2023): Is AI Music Composition Ready for Real Projects?

Most AI music tools in 2023 are stock-music-with-extra-steps: you pick a mood, you get a loop, you move on. AIVA is a different animal. It is built as a *composition* tool — it writes a score, hands you the MIDI, and lets you take it apart. That single design decision makes it far more useful than its competitors for some people, and completely unnecessary for others.

I spent a couple of weeks generating tracks for video projects, a podcast intro, and a game prototype to see where the line actually falls.

## What AIVA Is

AIVA (Artificial Intelligence Virtual Artist) is a Luxembourg-based AI composer that has been around considerably longer than most of the current crop of generative music tools. It generates instrumental pieces from style presets rather than from free-form text descriptions, then gives you an editor and export options on top.

The workflow has three entry points:

- **Preset styles** — pick from a library of genres (cinematic, ambient, electronic, jazz, tango, modern classical, and dozens more), set a duration and key, and generate.
- **Chord progression** — supply a progression and let AIVA compose around it.
- **Influence upload** — feed it a MIDI or audio file and it produces something in a similar vein.

You then get a set of variations, an editor for tweaking them, and downloads in MP3, WAV, or MIDI depending on your plan.

## Key Features

**MIDI and stem export.** This is the headline. Downloading a track as MIDI means you can drop it into Logic, Ableton, or Reaper and replace AIVA's stock instruments with your own libraries. The AI becomes a co-writer handling the blank-page problem, not a vending machine. For anyone who already produces music, this is the difference between usable and disposable.

**A real editor.** The in-browser editor exposes the individual tracks of a composition. You can mute a string section, change the instrument on a lead line, adjust tempo, and regenerate specific sections rather than rerolling the whole piece. It is not a full DAW and does not pretend to be, but it is far more control than "generate again and hope."

**Style breadth in orchestral and cinematic territory.** AIVA is noticeably stronger at orchestral, ambient, and cinematic material than at anything groove-driven. Its string writing and pad textures hold up. Its attempts at hip-hop and modern pop production do not.

**Duration control.** You can specify how long a piece should be, which matters enormously for video work where you need exactly 47 seconds under a segment. Most generators give you a fixed clip length and leave the trimming to you.

**Licensing tiers that actually address ownership.** Unlike a lot of generative tools that leave rights ambiguous, AIVA is explicit: what you can do with a track depends on your plan, up to and including full copyright transfer.

## Pros

- MIDI export makes the output genuinely editable rather than take-it-or-leave-it
- Strong at cinematic, ambient, and classical styles — the ones most video creators actually need
- Precise duration control fits real editing timelines
- Clear, tiered licensing including full ownership on the top plan
- Fast: a usable 90-second cue takes a couple of minutes end to end
- Generous style library that gives you a real starting point instead of one generic "corporate" mood

## Cons and Limitations

**No vocals, no lyrics, no natural-language prompting.** You cannot type "melancholy indie folk about a long drive home" and get a song. You pick a style preset and tune parameters. If you came expecting the conversational experience you get from a chat interface like [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review/) or an image tool like [Stable Diffusion](/reviews/stable-diffusion-review/), the workflow will feel dated.

**Output is competent, not distinctive.** The compositions are structurally sound and rarely embarrassing, but they also rarely surprise you. Melodies tend toward the safe. For background scoring that is exactly right; for anything meant to be the focus of attention, it shows.

**Stock instrument sounds are the weak link.** The composition is often better than the rendering. Playback through AIVA's built-in sounds can feel thin, especially on brass and percussion. This is precisely why MIDI export matters — the same piece run through decent sample libraries sounds dramatically better.

**Weak outside its comfort zone.** Anything requiring rhythmic feel, swing, or production-forward genres tends to come out stiff.

**Free tier is restrictive by design.** Three downloads a month and no commercial rights means the free plan is an evaluation tool, not a workflow.

## Who It's For

**Good fit:**

- Video editors and YouTubers who need original background scoring and are tired of the same handful of royalty-free library tracks
- Indie game developers needing looping ambient and menu music on a budget
- Producers who want a fast melodic or harmonic starting point they will rebuild in their own DAW
- Anyone who needs a cue at an exact length and does not want to fight a stock library

**Poor fit:**

- Anyone who needs songs with vocals
- Producers working in beat-driven or production-heavy genres
- People who want a text-prompt-and-done experience
- Casual users who only need a handful of tracks a year — a stock library subscription is cheaper

## How It Compares

Against Soundraw and Boomy, AIVA trades ease of use for control. Soundraw's interface is faster for grabbing a finished loop; Boomy is friendlier for absolute beginners. Neither gives you MIDI you can meaningfully rebuild. Mubert leans toward endless generative streams rather than discrete compositions. If you want a broader survey of the space, our roundup of the [best AI music generators](/best-ai-music-generators/) covers the alternatives side by side.

The deciding question is simple: do you own a DAW and know how to use it? If yes, AIVA is the strongest option here because the MIDI export lets you finish the job properly. If no, a simpler tool will get you to a usable track faster.

## Verdict

AIVA is the most *musician-shaped* AI composition tool available right now. It treats generation as the first draft rather than the finished product, and the MIDI export backs that philosophy up in a way its competitors do not.

It is also clearly a product from an earlier generation of AI tooling. The preset-driven interface feels rigid next to what natural-language interfaces have trained everyone to expect, the built-in sounds undersell the compositions, and the output plays it safe. Nothing it produces will be mistaken for a human composer working at the top of their game.

For background scoring where "good and appropriate" beats "memorable," at a cost far below hiring a composer, AIVA earns its subscription. Start on the free tier to test whether its style library covers what you actually need, then jump straight to Pro if you plan to use anything commercially — the middle tier's attribution requirements make it awkward for client work.

**Rating: 3.5/5** — genuinely useful for its niche, held back by an aging interface and instrument sounds that don't do the compositions justice.
