Every podcaster eventually hits the same wall: the conversation was great, but the raw file is full of "um," "uh," false starts, lip smacks, and three-second silences while someone looks for a word. Fixing that by hand takes roughly as long as the episode itself, sometimes longer.

Cleanvoice AI is built for exactly that chore. You upload audio, it finds and removes filler sounds, stutters, mouth noises, and dead air, and it hands back a cleaner file. Over the past few weeks I ran solo episodes, a two-person remote interview, and a deliberately messy panel recording through it to see how close it gets to a careful human edit.

## What Cleanvoice Is

Cleanvoice is a web-based audio cleanup service for spoken-word content, mainly podcasts. It is not an editor in the Audacity or Descript sense. There is no timeline to drag clips around on. You upload, choose what you want removed, wait for processing, then download the result.

That narrow focus is the point. Cleanvoice assumes you already have a recording and maybe an editing workflow, and it takes over the repetitive, low-judgment part of the edit.

## Key Features

**Filler-word removal.** The headline feature. Cleanvoice detects "um," "uh," "ah," "er," and their equivalents in other supported languages, then cuts them out. In my English tests it caught the large majority of obvious fillers and handled sentence-start "ums" particularly well.

**Stutter and false-start removal.** Repeated syllables and "I, I, I think" stumbles can be trimmed. This is harder than filler detection, and results were more mixed: clean stutters went cleanly, but some restarts where the speaker changed direction mid-sentence were left alone. That is probably the right call.

**Mouth sounds.** Lip smacks, clicks, and wet-mouth noise get removed or reduced. This was the pleasant surprise. Mouth clicks are maddening to fix manually, and Cleanvoice handled most of them without leaving obvious holes.

**Dead-air shortening.** Long silences get shortened rather than deleted outright, so the conversation keeps some natural rhythm. You can control how aggressive this is.

**Multitrack support.** If you recorded each speaker on a separate track, which you should, you can upload the tracks together and Cleanvoice keeps them in sync while editing. This matters: cleaning tracks individually would knock them out of alignment.

**Edit-list export.** Instead of a flattened file, you can export the edit points so a supported audio editor can import them for review. For anyone who masters in a DAW, this is the most professional way to use the tool, because you stay in control of every cut.

**Background noise reduction.** There is a basic noise reduction option, but it is not the main event. For heavy cleanup, a dedicated enhancer like [Adobe Podcast Enhance Speech](/reviews/adobe-podcast-enhance-speech-review-2023-studio-audio-from-a-bad-mic/) or a mastering service like [Auphonic](/reviews/auphonic-review-2023-the-set-and-forget-audio-engineer-for-podcasters/) does more.

## How It Performed in Testing

The **solo episode**, recorded on a decent USB mic in a treated room, came back sounding natural. I found only a handful of fillers it missed and one place where it trimmed a pause I would have kept.

The **remote interview**, recorded as two local tracks, was where multitrack support paid off. Both tracks stayed aligned, and crosstalk did not confuse it much. The guest's habit of saying "so" at the start of every answer was mostly left in place, which I counted as a win: that "so" was doing work.

The **panel recording**, four people on one mixed track with overlapping speech, was the stress test, and it showed. With everyone on one channel, cutting one person's "um" sometimes clipped the tail of someone else's sentence. Cleanvoice did no worse than a human working on the same file, but a mixed track limits what any tool can do.

## Pros

- **Saves real hours.** On a typical hour-long interview, the manual filler-and-click pass I would normally do mostly went away.
- **Mouth-sound removal is genuinely good.** It handles the most tedious manual fix well.
- **Multitrack-aware.** It keeps separate speaker tracks synced through the edit.
- **Fits existing workflows.** Edit-list export lets DAW users review every cut instead of trusting a black box.
- **Almost no learning curve.** Upload, tick boxes, download.

## Cons and Limitations

- **Not an editor.** You cannot cut tangents, rearrange segments, or add music. You still need another tool for the real edit.
- **Over-editing is possible.** Aggressive settings make people sound clipped and slightly robotic. The defaults are sensible, but resist cranking everything to maximum.
- **Mixed tracks are a weak spot.** Overlapping speech on a single channel leads to clipped words.
- **Waiting on processing.** Long files take a while to come back, which slows quick-turnaround shows.
- **Another subscription.** If you already pay for Descript, which has its own filler-word removal, the overlap is hard to justify.

## Pricing

Cleanvoice sells processing time rather than seats. As of July 2023, pricing works roughly like this:

- **Free trial:** a small amount of free processing, enough to test an episode or two.
- **Subscription plans:** start at roughly $10 a month for a few hours of audio, with higher tiers adding more hours per month.
- **Pay-as-you-go:** one-off hour packs for shows that publish irregularly.

Hour-based pricing means a weekly one-hour show sits comfortably in the lower tiers, while a daily show or a network will move up quickly. Check the current pricing page before you subscribe, because plans and hour allowances change.

## Cleanvoice vs. the All-in-One Alternatives

The real question is usually whether you need a single-purpose tool at all.

- **Descript** removes filler words through its transcript and is a complete editor. If you edit there, Cleanvoice is mostly redundant. See our [Descript review](/reviews/descript-review-2026-the-easiest-way-to-edit-audio-and-video/) for the full picture.
- **Podcastle** bundles recording, cleanup, and editing in the browser. Our [Podcastle review](/reviews/podcastle-review-2023-an-all-in-one-podcast-studio-in-the-browser/) covers where that all-in-one approach holds up.
- **Auphonic** handles leveling, loudness, and noise but does not remove filler words. It pairs well with Cleanvoice: clean first, master second.

Cleanvoice makes the most sense when you edit in a traditional DAW and do not want to change how you work, or when you outsource editing and want to cut the editor's hours.

## Who It's For

**A good fit:**
- Interview podcasters who record separate tracks and edit in Audition, Reaper, Audacity, or similar
- Freelance editors who want to cut the cleanup pass and spend their time on structure
- Course creators and narrators with a strong filler habit

**Probably not worth it:**
- People already editing in Descript
- Shows recorded on one mixed track with lots of crosstalk
- Anyone who wants an all-in-one recording and editing studio

## Verdict

Cleanvoice does one unglamorous job well. It finds most fillers, handles mouth clicks better than I expected, and respects multitrack recordings. It is not magic: it can over-trim, it struggles with mixed panel audio, and it replaces only one part of the edit, not the whole thing.

If you edit in a DAW and spend an hour per episode hunting for ums and clicks, Cleanvoice will likely pay for itself quickly. Run it on moderate settings, review the cuts, and keep a human pass for pacing and content. If you already live in Descript, you can probably skip it.
