Microsoft has spent two years wiring Copilot into everything it ships — Windows, Edge, Office, Teams. The pitch is seductive: the AI that already lives where you work. After months of using the free assistant, Copilot Pro, and Microsoft 365 Copilot in real workflows, the reality is a product family that ranges from genuinely excellent to strangely undercooked, sometimes within the same app.

## What is Microsoft Copilot?

"Copilot" is really three products sharing a brand:

1. **Copilot (free)** — the consumer chat assistant at copilot.microsoft.com, in Windows, and on mobile. Redesigned late last year with a friendlier interface, voice conversations, and a "Think Deeper" option for harder questions that runs on OpenAI's reasoning models.
2. **Copilot Pro ($20/month)** — priority access to the latest models, higher limits for image generation, and — the real selling point — Copilot inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Outlook for Microsoft 365 Personal/Family subscribers.
3. **Microsoft 365 Copilot ($30/user/month)** — the enterprise version, which adds the crucial ingredient: grounding in your organization's data. It can search your mail, files, chats, and meetings, and it powers the standout feature of the entire lineup, Teams meeting recaps.

Under the hood, Copilot runs on OpenAI's GPT-4-class models. Raw conversational ability is therefore comparable to [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review/) — the differentiation is entirely in where it runs and what it can see.

## Key features

**Teams meeting recaps (M365 Copilot).** The best thing Microsoft has shipped in years. Join late and ask "what did I miss?"; after the call, get summaries, decisions, and action items attributed to the right people. It works, consistently, and for meeting-heavy roles it alone can justify the seat cost.

**Outlook triage and drafting.** "Summarize this thread" on a 30-message chain is reliably useful, and draft-with-tone controls are solid. Email is where LLMs earn their keep, and Copilot's advantage is not making you paste anything anywhere.

**Word drafting and rewriting.** Good for first drafts from an outline, strong at "rewrite this section more concisely," and it can pull from other documents you reference. Long-document coherence has limits — it's a section-level tool, not a book-level one.

**Copilot in Excel.** The most hyped and least finished. It handles formula suggestions and quick analysis of clean tables, but stumbles on real-world spreadsheets — merged cells, multiple tables per sheet, anything messy. It has improved steadily, but treat demos with skepticism.

**Windows and free-tier chat.** Perfectly competent everyday assistant with voice that's pleasant to use. Image generation is included. The "Copilot key" on new laptops is a shrug, but the assistant itself is a fine ChatGPT alternative at the same price: free.

## What works well

The pattern across the lineup: **Copilot is at its best when it summarizes things you already have, and weakest when it creates things from scratch.** Meeting recaps, inbox triage, "catch me up on this document" — these exploit Microsoft's actual moat, which is that your work already lives in its apps. No copy-paste, no context re-explaining, no separate tool. That integration advantage is real, and competitors piping the same models through a browser tab can't match it.

Enterprise controls are also a legitimate strength. Copilot respects existing file permissions, offers admin governance, and comes with commercial data protection — the boring plumbing that makes AI deployable in regulated organizations, and a big reason [enterprise adoption](/news/news-073-enterprise-ai-q4/) keeps tilting toward Microsoft.

## Cons and limitations

**Quality is uneven across apps.** Teams and Outlook Copilot feel finished; Excel and PowerPoint feel like previews. PowerPoint generates decks that are structurally fine and visually generic — you'll rebuild half the slides anyway.

**It's rarely the best model experience.** OpenAI and Anthropic ship their newest capabilities to their own apps first. Power users who care about frontier reasoning will still keep a [ChatGPT](/is-chatgpt-plus-worth-20-dollars/) or [Claude](/reviews/claude-review/) subscription alongside — which undermines the "one assistant" pitch.

**The pricing stack adds up.** M365 Copilot's $30/user/month lands on top of existing licensing, and Microsoft has also folded Copilot into consumer 365 subscriptions while nudging those prices upward. For a 200-person org, $72,000/year invites hard questions about which roles actually benefit.

**Context limits bite.** Ask it to synthesize across many long documents and it quietly truncates. It's grounded in your data, but not in *all* of your data at once.

**Trust wobbles linger.** The Recall episode on Windows last year — announced, criticized, delayed, reworked — left a mark on how much ambient access users want to grant. Copilot's permissions are more conservative, but the skepticism is earned and worth keeping.

## Pricing

As of March 2025, approximate pricing: free tier ($0), Copilot Pro at $20/month, Microsoft 365 Copilot at $30/user/month for business (annual commitment), with a free Copilot Chat tier for business users and pay-as-you-go agent features being layered on. Microsoft is visibly still experimenting with packaging, so check current terms.

## Who is it for?

**Get M365 Copilot if:** your organization lives in Teams and Outlook, and the seats go to meeting-heavy managers and coordinators first. Pilot with a specific group and measure, rather than blanket-licensing.

**Get Copilot Pro if:** you're a Microsoft 365 personal subscriber who writes and emails a lot in Office apps. If your work happens outside Office, plain [ChatGPT Plus](/is-chatgpt-plus-worth-20-dollars/) is the better $20.

**Stick with free Copilot if:** you want a capable general assistant with voice and images at zero cost — it's one of the best free options available.

## Verdict

Microsoft Copilot is two products wearing one name: a genuinely excellent meeting-and-email assistant that exploits Microsoft's unbeatable position inside your workday, and a still-maturing content creator that trails the standalone chatbots it's built from. Buy it for the summarization and triage; don't buy it expecting Excel magic or frontier-model reasoning. For the right roles, it's the rare enterprise AI purchase that visibly pays for itself — just not for every seat in the building.
