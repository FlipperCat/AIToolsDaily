The AI browser race got crowded fast. Perplexity shipped Comet, OpenAI shipped Atlas, Google folded Gemini deeper into Chrome — and The Browser Company, the team behind the much-loved Arc, bet everything on Dia. After several weeks of using Dia as my daily browser, my take is that it's the most approachable AI browser you can install right now, and also the least ambitious. Depending on who you are, that's either the selling point or the dealbreaker.

## What is Dia?

Dia is a Chromium-based Mac browser where the address bar doubles as a chat interface. Type a URL and it navigates; type a question and it answers — using the page you're on, the tabs you choose to reference, and (if you opt in) your recent browsing history as context.

It's the successor to Arc in spirit and in fact: The Browser Company moved Arc to maintenance mode in 2025 to focus on Dia, and the company was acquired by Atlassian later that year, a signal that Dia's future leans toward work and teams. The beta launched in mid-2025 as invite-only and Mac-only, and the Mac-first posture hasn't really changed.

Where Arc was a power tool that demanded you relearn browsing, Dia goes the opposite direction. It looks almost aggressively normal — horizontal tabs, familiar shortcuts, Chrome extension support. The AI is the only unusual thing about it, which is clearly the point.

## Key features

**Chat with your tabs.** This is the core loop. Hit the shortcut, ask a question, and Dia answers using the current page. Type `@` to pull other open tabs into the conversation — "compare @pricing-page-a with @pricing-page-b" — and it synthesizes across them. It's the same fundamental idea as Comet's sidecar, but the @-mention model makes multi-tab questions feel more deliberate and less magical-black-box. In practice I found it the single most pleasant implementation of this pattern I've used.

**Skills.** Skills are reusable prompts you build in plain language — "summarize this paper in bullet points with the methodology called out" or "rewrite this in our support voice" — and then invoke by name. You can share them, and there's a gallery of prebuilt ones. It's a lightweight take on custom agents, and because Skills are just structured prompts, non-technical people actually make them. This is Dia's most underrated feature.

**Memory and personalization.** Opt in, and Dia uses your recent browsing to personalize answers — it knows what you've been researching and tailors responses accordingly. It's genuinely useful ("what were those three flight options I looked at yesterday?") and genuinely something you should think about before enabling. The controls for what's included are reasonable, and it's off by default.

**Inline writing help.** Summon the assistant in any text field to draft, rewrite, or tighten. Table stakes for the category at this point, but Dia's implementation is fast and doesn't hijack your cursor.

**Chromium underneath.** Extensions, password managers, and site compatibility all behave like Chrome. Importing from Chrome or Arc takes minutes.

## What works well

Dia nails the on-ramp. My non-technical test (installing it for a family member who has never touched a chatbot settings page) went better than with any other AI browser: within a day they were asking questions about recipes and news articles without being taught. The address-bar-as-chat metaphor requires zero new habits.

The @-mention tab referencing is the best version of multi-tab context I've used. Comet can see your tabs too, but explicitly naming which tabs matter produces noticeably more focused answers and fewer "which tab did it just read?" moments. For research workflows — comparing documentation pages, cross-referencing sources — this alone justifies the install. If you live in Perplexity's ecosystem instead, our [Comet review](/reviews/perplexity-comet-browser-review-2026/) covers the tradeoff in detail.

Skills quietly compound. After a few weeks I had a half-dozen that I use daily, and they've replaced a folder of saved prompts I used to paste into [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-plus-review-2026/).

## Cons and limitations

**It barely does agentic tasks.** Dia answers questions about pages; it doesn't really operate the browser for you. Comet and Atlas will fill forms, navigate flows, and complete multi-step tasks (with mixed reliability, to be fair). Dia's restraint means fewer prompt-injection nightmares, but if "the browser does the errand for me" is what you want from an AI browser, Dia isn't that product yet.

**Mac-only.** In 2026 this remains a real limitation. Windows users are simply out of luck for now.

**Arc refugees will miss things.** No Spaces, no vertical tab sidebar, far less customization. Dia is intentionally simpler, and Arc power users feel every removed feature.

**The answer engine is only as good as its context.** When Dia doesn't have the right page open, its answers fall back to generic model knowledge, and it's less transparent about sourcing than a dedicated answer engine like [Perplexity](/reviews/perplexity-ai-review-2026/). I caught a few confidently wrong summaries of long PDFs — skim before you trust.

**Post-acquisition uncertainty.** Atlassian ownership suggests a work-and-teams direction. That could mean great Jira and Confluence integration; it could also mean the consumer product plays second fiddle. Too early to say.

## Pricing

As of August 2026: a free tier with capped AI usage, and Dia Pro at roughly $20/month for unlimited assistant use. The cap on the free tier is workable for light use — a handful of conversations a day — but anyone who adopts the chat-with-tabs habit will hit it. Prices and limits change often in this category, so check the current page before deciding.

Against the competition: Comet's core assistant is free with paid tiers for heavier tasks, and Atlas rides on your existing ChatGPT subscription. Dia Pro's $20 is defensible but is also a second AI subscription for most people, and that math is getting harder to justify across the board.

## Who is it for?

**Choose Dia if:** you're on a Mac, you want AI help while reading and writing rather than an agent that acts for you, you value a gentle learning curve, or you're setting up a less technical person with their first AI browser. It's also the most natural landing spot for ex-Arc users who want to stay in the family.

**Skip it if:** you're on Windows, you want agentic task automation, or you need Arc-style power-user tab management. And if you just want AI summaries occasionally, a browser extension plus your existing chatbot subscription gets you 70% of this for free.

## Verdict

Dia is the AI browser I recommend to the most people and evangelize the least. It has made one clear bet — that most people want a friendly browser that can talk about what they're reading, not a semi-autonomous agent — and it executes that bet with more polish than anyone else. The Skills system is quietly excellent, the @-mention model is the right interface for tab context, and the whole thing feels finished in a way this category usually doesn't.

But the ceiling is visible. Comet and Atlas are racing toward browsers that do things, and Dia is still a browser that discusses things. If The Browser Company can layer real capability on top of this foundation without breaking its approachability — and if Atlassian lets it — Dia could win. Right now it's the best AI browser for normal people, and merely a pleasant one for power users.
