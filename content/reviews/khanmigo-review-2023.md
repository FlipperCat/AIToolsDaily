When OpenAI announced GPT-4 in March, one of the launch partners got more attention from parents and teachers than any other: Khan Academy. Its new AI assistant, **Khanmigo**, promised something ChatGPT explicitly doesn't: a tutor that refuses to do your homework for you.

Four months in, Khanmigo is still in a waitlisted pilot, but we've had enough time with it to judge whether the idea holds up. Short version: the tutoring approach is excellent, the teacher tools are genuinely useful, and the math reliability isn't quite where it needs to be.

## What Khanmigo Is

Khanmigo is an AI assistant built into Khan Academy's existing platform of exercises, videos, and articles. It runs on GPT-4 with a large layer of Khan Academy prompting, guardrails, and product design on top. There are two main modes:

- **For learners:** a Socratic tutor across math, science, humanities, coding, and writing.
- **For teachers:** an assistant that drafts lesson plans, hooks, rubrics, exit tickets, and differentiated activities.

The core design choice sets it apart from a plain [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review/) session: Khanmigo is instructed *not* to give away answers. Instead it asks what you've tried, points out where your reasoning went wrong, and nudges you toward the next step.

## Key Features

### Socratic Tutoring

Stuck on a quadratic? Khanmigo asks you to identify the coefficients first, then what formula might help. If you get a step wrong, it asks you to check that step rather than correcting it outright. This is closer to how a good human tutor works, and it is the product's biggest strength.

### Context from Khan Academy Exercises

When you open Khanmigo alongside a Khan Academy exercise, it knows which problem you're on. You don't need to retype the question, and the tutoring lines up with the curriculum you're already following.

### Writing Coach

For essays, Khanmigo helps with brainstorming, outlining, and feedback on drafts, but it won't write the essay. Teachers can see the conversation history, which addresses a big concern about AI writing tools in classrooms.

### Learning Activities

Some of the more creative features include debate practice, where you argue a position against the AI, and conversations with simulated historical and literary figures. These are fun and can spark engagement, though they work best as discussion starters, not sources of fact.

### Teacher Tools

For educators, Khanmigo can generate lesson plans aligned to a topic, write discussion questions, suggest ways to differentiate for struggling or advanced students, and draft rubrics. If you've been doing this with a general chatbot (see our guide to [writing lesson plans with ChatGPT](/tutorials/write-lesson-plans-with-chatgpt-2023/)), Khanmigo's versions are more classroom-shaped out of the box.

### Oversight and Safety

Student conversations are logged and visible to linked teachers and parents. Khanmigo also flags potentially harmful content. For schools, this transparency is a big reason to prefer it over letting students use an open chatbot.

## Pros

- **Pedagogically sound.** The refusal to give answers is a feature, not a bug. It rewards understanding.
- **Built on GPT-4.** Reasoning and explanations are noticeably better than models built on GPT-3.5. Our [GPT-4 vs GPT-3.5 comparison](/compare/gpt-4-vs-gpt-3-5-2023/) explains the gap.
- **Integrated with a trusted curriculum.** It works alongside Khan Academy's existing exercises instead of floating free.
- **Transparent to adults.** Parents and teachers can review what students asked.
- **Helpful teacher assistant.** Saves real prep time on routine planning tasks.

## Cons and Limitations

- **Math errors happen.** Early testers, including us, have caught arithmetic slips and occasionally flawed reasoning. That's a serious problem for a math tutor, because students may trust a wrong correction.
- **Access is limited.** It's a waitlisted, US-focused pilot. Many interested families and schools can't get in yet.
- **Can be slow.** GPT-4 responses take noticeably longer than typical chatbots, which tests younger students' patience.
- **Can feel rigid.** Students who already understand a concept and just want to check an answer may find the constant questioning tedious.
- **Historical figure chats need framing.** They're simulations and can put inaccurate or anachronistic words in famous mouths.

## Pricing (approximate, as of July 2023)

- **Learners and parents (US):** access through a donation to Khan Academy, around **$9/month or $99/year** during the pilot, with a waitlist.
- **Schools and districts:** available through district partnerships, with pricing arranged directly with Khan Academy.
- **Teachers:** access so far comes mainly through participating districts.

Khan Academy has said the pilot pricing reflects the high cost of running GPT-4. Expect the model and pricing to change as the program expands.

## How It Compares to General Chatbots

A general assistant like ChatGPT or Google Bard (see our [Bard vs ChatGPT comparison](/compare/bard-vs-chatgpt-2023/)) is more flexible and will happily solve a problem outright. That's exactly the problem for education: it's too easy to skip the learning. Khanmigo is less flexible by design, and for students, that's the point.

For teachers, a general chatbot can do most of what Khanmigo's teacher tools do, given good prompts. Khanmigo's advantage is that the output starts out classroom-ready, and student usage stays visible and safe.

## Who It's For

**Good fit:**
- Parents who want a patient, always-available homework helper that won't simply hand over answers.
- Students motivated to understand material, especially in middle and high school math and science.
- Teachers in participating districts looking to speed up lesson prep.

**Poor fit:**
- Students who need guaranteed-accurate answer checking.
- Families outside the US or anyone who can't get off the waitlist.
- Learners who find back-and-forth questioning frustrating rather than helpful.

## Tips for Parents and Teachers

1. **Treat it as a tutor, not an answer key.** Encourage kids to verify final answers against the exercise's own checking.
2. **Review conversations occasionally.** It's a good way to spot misunderstandings, and the rare wrong AI correction.
3. **Use historical chats as prompts for discussion**, not as research sources.
4. **Teachers: edit before you use.** AI-drafted lesson plans are a starting point, not a finished product.

## Verdict

Khanmigo is the most thoughtful attempt yet to put a large language model in front of students. The Socratic design addresses the biggest worry about AI in education, which is students skipping the learning, and the oversight features make it easier for schools to adopt. The weak points are real: occasional math mistakes, limited access, and slow responses.

**Rating: 7.5/10.** It's the right idea, well executed on design and still maturing on reliability. If you can get into the pilot, it's worth trying, with an adult checking in now and then.
