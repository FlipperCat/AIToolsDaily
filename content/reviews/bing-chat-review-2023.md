Microsoft shipped the new Bing just over two weeks ago, and it has already produced more headlines than the previous decade of Bing combined — some of them the kind a product team does not want. Underneath the drama is a genuinely interesting product: a search engine that answers in prose, cites its sources, and occasionally argues with you about what year it is.

I've been using the preview daily since access came through. Here's an honest read on where it helps, where it embarrasses itself, and whether it's worth the waitlist.

## What Bing Chat Is

Bing Chat is a conversational layer built on top of Bing search, powered by an OpenAI model that Microsoft describes as more capable than the one behind ChatGPT, wired into live search results through an orchestration layer Microsoft calls Prometheus.

The practical difference from ChatGPT is the wiring. When you ask a question, Bing runs actual searches, reads the results, and composes an answer with numbered footnotes linking back to the pages it used. You get a synthesized answer *and* a path to verify it. That verification path is the entire product thesis, and it's a good one.

Access is by waitlist. Signing in with a Microsoft account, setting Bing as your default search, and installing the mobile app reportedly moves you up the queue faster.

## Key Features

**Cited answers.** Every claim in a response carries a footnote. This sounds small and isn't. It's the difference between "an AI told me" and "here are three sources, one of which is the vendor's own documentation." When Bing is wrong, you can usually catch it in one click.

**Live web access.** Ask about a product launched last week, a schedule, or a price, and Bing looks it up. [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review/) simply can't do that — its knowledge stops at a training cutoff, and it will confidently invent the rest.

**The Edge sidebar.** Chat sits alongside whatever page you're reading and can answer questions about it. Summarize a long PDF, ask what a dense paragraph means, compare a spec sheet against a competitor's. This is the feature I use most and the one that's hardest to replicate elsewhere.

**Compose.** A drafting tool in the sidebar with controls for tone, format, and length. Pick "professional," "email," "short," give it a one-line prompt, and it produces a serviceable draft you can drop into the page you're on. Not brilliant writing, but a real time-saver for routine correspondence.

**Follow-up suggestions.** Bing proposes next questions under each answer. They're often better than what I would have asked, which is either helpful or mildly insulting depending on the day.

## Pros

- **Sources you can check**, which makes it usable for research rather than just for drafting
- **Current information**, including things that happened this week
- **Free during preview**, with no subscription equivalent to ChatGPT Plus required
- **The sidebar workflow** genuinely changes how you read long documents
- **Good at comparison tasks** — "compare these three laptops on battery life and weight" produces a clean table with links

## Cons and Limitations

**It still makes things up.** Citations reduce hallucination; they don't eliminate it. I've watched Bing cite a real page and then summarize a claim that page doesn't make. The footnote creates an illusion of rigor that occasionally isn't there, and that's arguably more dangerous than an uncited wrong answer.

**The conversations went strange.** Extended sessions during the first two weeks produced widely reported exchanges where the model became defensive, insistent, or oddly emotional. Microsoft's response was to cap conversation length — initially five turns per session and fifty per day, since loosened slightly with more increases promised. It's a real fix for a real failure mode, but it means you cannot work a problem over twenty messages the way you can in ChatGPT.

**It's slow.** Searching, reading, and composing takes noticeably longer than a plain search or a ChatGPT reply. For a quick factual lookup, ten blue links still win.

**Edge lock-in.** The best parts are browser features, and Microsoft is not subtle about the trade. If you don't want Edge, you're getting a partial product.

**Waitlist friction.** Weeks of demand and a staged rollout mean plenty of people reading about it still can't use it.

## Pricing

Free, as of late February 2023, for anyone with a Microsoft account who has cleared the waitlist. There's no paid tier and no announced plan for one. Compare that with ChatGPT Plus at roughly $20/month for priority access — for search-shaped questions, Bing is currently the better deal simply by costing nothing.

Assume this changes. A search engine is an advertising business, and a chat interface with no blue links is an advertising business with a hole in it. How Microsoft resolves that is the interesting question of the next year.

## Who It's For

**Good fit:** researchers and analysts who need sourced answers; anyone doing product comparisons or purchase research; heavy readers who want a sidebar that can summarize and interrogate a page; people who found ChatGPT useful but were burned by its outdated or invented facts.

**Poor fit:** anyone doing long, iterative creative or coding work, where the turn cap is a constant interruption; people committed to Chrome or Safari; teams needing predictable, auditable output, since preview behavior is still shifting week to week.

If sourced AI answers are the appeal, [Perplexity](/reviews/perplexity-review/) is worth a look too — it launched with the same citation-first idea and no waitlist. Our [ChatGPT vs Perplexity comparison](/compare/chatgpt-vs-perplexity/) covers that split, and [Perplexity vs Google](/compare/perplexity-vs-google/) looks at whether any of this beats a plain search box yet.

## Verdict

Bing Chat is the most interesting thing to happen to search in a very long time, and it is unmistakably a preview. The citation model is right, the sidebar is genuinely useful, and the live-web grounding fixes ChatGPT's most annoying weakness. Against that: it's slow, it's capped, it occasionally fabricates behind a footnote, and it wants your browser.

Join the waitlist. Use it for research and reading. Don't retire your ability to check things.

**Rating: 3.8/5** — a strong idea, shipped early, still visibly finding its footing.
