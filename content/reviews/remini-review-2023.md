Remini had a strange summer. An app that spent years quietly sharpening blurry photos suddenly took over TikTok and Instagram when people discovered its AI Photos feature: upload a handful of selfies, pick a style, and get back studio-style headshots. Professional headshots went viral, and so did the "what would our baby look like" filter.

Viral is not the same as good. We spent several weeks testing Remini on what it was originally built for (old, blurry, and low-resolution photos) and on the headshot feature that made it famous. Here's what holds up, what doesn't, and whether the subscription makes sense.

## What Remini is

Remini is a mobile-first AI photo and video enhancer, with a web version for desktop use. Its core job is face restoration: take a soft, noisy, or tiny photo and rebuild the face so it looks sharp at a modern resolution. It's now part of the Bending Spoons app portfolio and is sold mainly as a subscription.

The app has three main jobs:

1. **Enhance** blurry, old, or compressed photos, mostly faces.
2. **Enhance video**, applying the same approach frame by frame to short clips.
3. **AI Photos**, which builds a personal model from your selfies and generates stylized portraits.

## Key features

### Face enhancement

This is still Remini's strongest feature. Feed it a blurry phone photo from a dark restaurant, or a scanned print from the 1980s, and the faces come back crisp, with defined eyes, clean edges, and realistic-looking skin. On small images where the face is only a few dozen pixels wide, the jump in quality is dramatic.

The catch is how it gets there. Remini isn't recovering detail that was hidden in the file. It's predicting what a face like that probably looked like. Most of the time the guess is close. Sometimes it isn't: we saw eye colors shift, teeth get "fixed," and an older relative lose a decade of wrinkles.

### Background and color tools

Remini can also enhance the background and add color to black-and-white photos. Background enhancement is weaker than face work. Backgrounds tend to look painted rather than sharpened. Colorization is a fun preview, but skin tones and clothing colors are guesses, and it shows.

### Video enhance

Video enhancement works on short clips and is slow, with more obvious artifacts than stills. Faces can flicker between frames as the model makes slightly different guesses. It's fine for a few seconds of old home video you want to post. It's not a restoration tool for anything longer.

### AI Photos

This is the viral feature. You upload a set of selfies (more variety in lighting, angle, and expression gives better results), Remini trains on them, and you choose from themed styles: professional headshot, fantasy, decade looks, and so on.

The results are mixed in a predictable way:

- **Good:** A handful of images per batch look polished and recognizably you. The professional headshot style is the most useful in practice.
- **Bad:** A large share of each batch drifts. Faces get slimmed, skin gets airbrushed, and some users have reported changes to body shape or skin tone that they didn't ask for. Hands, collars, and earrings are frequent failure points.

If you've tried [Lensa's Magic Avatars](/reviews/lensa-ai-review-2023-are-magic-avatars-worth-the-hype-and-the-privacy-trade-off/), the experience is similar. Remini leans more toward realistic photography, while Lensa leans more toward illustration.

## Pros

- **Excellent at small, blurry faces.** It's one of the best one-tap fixes for low-resolution portraits we've tested.
- **Very simple.** No settings to learn. Upload, wait, download.
- **AI Photos can deliver a usable headshot** for people who need one quickly and don't want to book a photographer.
- **Web version** makes batch work on a laptop less painful than doing it on a phone.

## Cons and limitations

- **It invents detail.** That's how the technology works, but it matters for family history, legal, or journalistic photos. The enhanced image is an interpretation, not a recovery.
- **Over-smoothing.** The default look trends toward plastic skin. There's limited control over enhancement strength.
- **Subscription pressure.** The free tier is mainly a trial with ads. The paid plan is priced weekly, which adds up fast if you forget to cancel.
- **Faces only, mostly.** For non-face problems such as removing objects or fixing backgrounds, a dedicated tool like [Cleanup.pictures](/reviews/cleanup.pictures-review-2023-the-fastest-way-to-erase-things-from-photos/) does a cleaner job.
- **Privacy questions.** AI Photos asks for a set of clear photos of your face. Read the data policy first, and don't upload photos of people who haven't agreed to it.

## Pricing (approximate, as of October 2023)

Remini's pricing varies by platform and region, and the app often shows different offers to different users. Expect roughly:

- **Free:** A limited number of enhancements with ads and slower processing.
- **Remini Pro:** A weekly subscription, typically in the single-digit dollars per week, with longer plans offered at a discount. It removes ads, raises limits, and unlocks more AI Photos styles.

Check the exact price in your app store before you subscribe. Weekly billing is the main trap here. If you need Remini for one project, set a reminder to cancel. Prices change often, so treat these figures as a guide.

## How it compares

- **Versus Lensa:** Lensa's avatars are more artistic. Remini's AI Photos aim for realistic portraits and handle "make this look like a professional headshot" better.
- **Versus desktop editors:** Photoshop's neural filters and dedicated upscalers give you far more control over strength and blending. They take more skill and cost more up front.
- **Versus free image generators:** Tools like [Playground AI](/reviews/playground-ai-review-2023-the-most-generous-free-image-generator/) can create stylized portraits too, but they don't train on your face. Remini's advantage is likeness.

## Who it's for

**Good fit:**

- People with a phone full of blurry photos of friends and family who want shareable versions.
- Anyone who needs a decent headshot this week and can accept sorting through a batch.
- Social media users who want quick before-and-after results.

**Not a good fit:**

- Archivists and genealogists who need faithful restoration. Use Remini for a preview, and keep the originals.
- Professional photographers who need control over every adjustment.
- Anyone uncomfortable uploading clear face photos to a cloud service.

## Tips for better results

1. **Start with the best source you have.** A higher-resolution scan of an old print beats a phone photo of that print.
2. **Crop to the face before enhancing** when the subject is small in the frame. It gives the model more to work with.
3. **For AI Photos, vary everything:** lighting, angle, background, and expression. Ten nearly identical selfies give you ten nearly identical mistakes.
4. **Compare at 100% zoom** before you share. Look at eyes, teeth, ears, and hairlines, where errors are most common.

## Verdict

Remini is very good at one thing: turning small, blurry faces into sharp, shareable portraits in seconds. It is also good at AI headshots, as long as you accept a lot of rejects. It's weaker as a general photo editor, it makes confident guesses that aren't always faithful, and its weekly pricing deserves caution.

Use it for quick improvements to casual photos and for an emergency headshot. Don't use it as an archival tool. Before you upload anything, decide whether you're comfortable with the privacy terms.

**Rating: 3.8 / 5** for its core enhancement quality, with points off for pricing and accuracy.
