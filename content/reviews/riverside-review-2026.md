Remote podcast recording has one fundamental problem: the internet. Record over a call and your audio quality is capped by the worst connection in the room. Riverside's answer — record locally on every participant's device at full quality, then upload the files in the background — is now the standard approach, and Riverside is the platform that made it mainstream.

Four years into using it on and off, the recording engine remains excellent. The editing suite that has been built on top of it is where opinions diverge.

## What Riverside Is

Riverside is a browser-based recording studio for podcasts and video interviews. Each participant joins through a link, sees a normal video call, and — crucially — has their audio and video captured locally at up to 4K video and uncompressed 48kHz audio rather than at whatever the call bandwidth allowed.

Around that core it has accumulated a full production layer: transcription, a text-based editor, AI-generated short clips, AI show notes, a teleprompter, a soundboard for intros and stingers, live streaming to social platforms, and mobile apps for recording on the move.

The pitch is that you record, edit, and publish without leaving one tab. For a lot of shows, that now holds up.

## Key Features

**Local recording with progressive upload.** The headline feature and still the reason to choose it. Files upload chunk by chunk during the session, so you are not waiting on a giant transfer at the end and you are not losing everything if someone's browser crashes at minute fifty.

**Separate tracks per participant.** Every speaker gets an isolated audio file and an isolated video file. This is non-negotiable for serious editing — it lets you fix one person's levels, cut around a cough, or reframe a single speaker without touching anyone else.

**Text-based editing.** Riverside transcribes the session and lets you cut by deleting text, the same interaction model that made [Descript](/reviews/04-descript-review/) popular. It handles filler-word removal, gap tightening, and speaker-view layouts.

**AI clip generation.** Riverside scans the recording for self-contained moments and produces vertical short-form clips with captions. The selection is decent rather than brilliant — it reliably finds *a* good moment and reliably misses the best one. Treat it as a first pass that saves you scrubbing, not as a replacement for judgement.

**AI show notes and transcripts.** Summaries, chapter suggestions, and titles generated from the transcript, plus transcription across a wide range of languages. Serviceable output that needs editing before publishing.

**Audio enhancement.** One-click cleanup that reduces room noise and evens out levels. Effective on mediocre microphones, occasionally over-processed on good ones.

**Streaming and live audience.** You can broadcast the session live to social platforms while still capturing the high-quality local files, and bring in call-in listeners.

## What It Gets Right

**The recording quality is genuinely the best-in-class version of this idea.** When a session completes normally, the files you get back sound like everyone was in the same studio. That is the entire value proposition and it delivers.

**Guests do not need to do anything.** No app install, no account, no settings to configure. You send a link, they click it, they allow the microphone. Every friction point you remove from a guest's path is a guest who actually shows up on time.

**The end-to-end workflow saves real hours.** Recording, rough cut, clips, transcript, and show notes in one place removes three or four file handoffs per episode. For a weekly show, that compounds.

**Separate-track exports keep your options open.** Even if you never use the built-in editor, you can pull clean isolated tracks and finish in whatever tool you prefer. You are not locked in.

## Cons and Limitations

**The editor is good, not deep.** It handles interview shows well. The moment you want layered graphics, precise timeline control, or anything cinematic, you will export and finish elsewhere. Riverside knows this and does not really pretend otherwise, but the marketing implies more completeness than the editor delivers.

**Uploads can strand you.** The local-recording model has a failure mode: if a guest closes the browser before their upload finishes, that track may be incomplete. It is recoverable more often than not, but "more often than not" is an uncomfortable phrase for a recording you cannot redo. Always tell guests to keep the tab open until you say so.

**It is browser- and hardware-hungry.** Recording 4K locally while running a video call is demanding. Older laptops get hot, fans get loud, and fans get recorded. Guests on underpowered machines are the most common source of problems.

**AI features are helpful but generic.** The clips, titles, and summaries all land in a competent middle. They are worth having as a starting point and they are not worth publishing unedited. If transcription accuracy is your primary need rather than a byproduct, a dedicated tool like [Otter](/reviews/16-otter-ai-review/) is still stronger on that one dimension.

**Cost scales awkwardly for small shows.** Recording hours are metered, and a show that records long sessions and cuts them down burns through allowances faster than the runtime of the published episode suggests.

## Pricing

Approximate as of July 2026, billed annually:

- **Free** — a couple of hours of recording per month with limited exports. Enough to evaluate.
- **Standard** — roughly $15/month. Separate tracks, 4K, a modest monthly hour allowance.
- **Pro** — roughly $24/month. More hours, longer AI clip allowances, custom branding.
- **Business** — roughly $32/user/month. Team seats, admin controls, higher limits.

Prices, allowances, and tier names in this category change often — verify current plans before subscribing. The practical guidance: estimate your monthly raw recording hours, not your published hours, and pick the tier from that number.

## Who It's For

**Interview podcasters with remote guests.** This is the core use case and Riverside is the safest choice in it.

**Video-first shows.** If you publish to YouTube as well as audio platforms, the multi-track 4K capture plus built-in clip generation covers a workflow that would otherwise need three tools.

**Teams recording customer interviews or internal content.** The zero-install guest experience matters more inside organisations than it does for consumer podcasts.

**Who should look elsewhere:** solo creators recording alone into a local mic — you do not need remote recording infrastructure, and a straightforward editor will cost less. Shows with heavy post-production also gain less here, since the editing layer is the weakest part. Our [Descript vs Riverside comparison](/compare/descript-vs-riverside-2024/) walks through that trade-off in more detail, and it still holds: Riverside wins on capture, Descript wins on editing.

## Verdict

Riverside remains the best remote recording platform available, and the AI production layer built on top of it has moved from gimmick to genuinely time-saving. If your show depends on remote guests, the quality difference against recording over a call is not subtle.

The caveats are real, though. The editor will not be your last stop if you care about polish, the AI output needs a human pass before publishing, and the local-recording model asks you to trust guests to keep a tab open. None of these are dealbreakers; all of them are worth knowing before you build a production process around the tool.

**Recommendation:** use the free tier to run two real sessions with actual guests on their actual hardware. The recording quality will sell itself. Then decide, based on how much editing you really do, whether you need Riverside alone or Riverside plus a proper editor downstream.
