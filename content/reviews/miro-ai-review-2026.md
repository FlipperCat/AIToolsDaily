Miro has spent a decade being the default infinite whiteboard for remote teams, which means it has also been the default place where good ideas go to die. Every company has boards from workshops nobody ever synthesized — 200 stickies in six colors, three orphaned frames, and a title like "Q2 Discovery FINAL v3."

Miro AI is aimed squarely at that. Not at replacing the workshop, but at handling the tedious work on either side of it: generating the starting structure, then turning the sprawl back into something readable. I have run it through a few real sessions — a discovery workshop, a retro, and some solo diagramming — to see where it earns its keep.

## What Miro AI Is

Miro AI is not a separate product. It is a set of features layered into the canvas you already use, invoked either from the toolbar or by selecting objects and picking an action. There is no chat window to babysit and no separate app to learn, which is the right design choice for a tool people open in the middle of a live call.

The features fall into three groups: **generate** (create diagrams, mind maps, images, and stickies from a prompt), **organize** (cluster and tag existing content), and **synthesize** (summarize a selection or a whole board into text). Usage is metered with AI credits tied to your plan, so it is not an unlimited buffet.

## Key Features

**Sticky-note clustering.** Select a swarm of stickies, run the cluster action, and Miro groups them by theme with generated group labels. This is the single most useful thing in the product. Post-workshop affinity mapping normally eats the last 15 minutes of a session — or gets skipped entirely, which is worse. The grouping is not always how a facilitator would do it, but dragging three stickies to a different cluster is much faster than building the map from scratch.

**Board and selection summaries.** Point it at a frame and get a paragraph or bulleted digest. Useful for the person who missed the session, and useful for pasting into the follow-up doc so the board is not the only record.

**Diagram generation from a prompt.** Describe a process — "onboarding flow for a self-serve SaaS trial, with a branch for teams that need SSO" — and get a connected diagram with real Miro shapes you can edit, not a flat image. Quality is solid for linear flows and org charts, shakier for anything with dense cross-connections.

**Mind maps from a topic.** Fast expansion of a central idea into branches. Handy for kicking off a solo planning session; less useful with a group, because the whole point of a group mind map is the arguing.

**AI image generation on canvas.** Fine for placeholder visuals and mood-boarding. Nobody should be shipping these.

**Prototype and code helpers.** Miro will take a wireframe frame and produce starter markup, and can turn boards into rough presentations. Both are convenience features rather than reasons to buy — the markup needs a developer's hands on it before it becomes anything.

## Pros

- **Zero learning curve.** Everything is a right-click or toolbar action on content you already have. Nobody needs training.
- **Clustering genuinely saves facilitator time.** This is the feature I would actually miss.
- **Output is native canvas objects.** Generated diagrams are editable shapes and connectors, so the AI does not create a dead end you have to redo by hand.
- **It works live.** Running a cluster mid-workshop while people watch is fast enough to not kill the room's energy.
- **No workflow change required.** If your team is already in Miro, adoption cost is essentially zero.

## Cons and Limitations

- **Credit limits are the real constraint.** The allowance on lower tiers runs out faster than you expect once a team of eight starts poking at it, and the metering is opaque enough that people ration it nervously.
- **Summaries are bland.** They accurately describe what is on the board and stubbornly refuse to say anything about it. Do not expect insight, prioritization, or "here is the tension between clusters three and five."
- **Diagram layout needs cleanup.** Shapes are right, connectors are mostly right, spacing and labels are usually not. Budget a few minutes of tidying.
- **Struggles with messy real boards.** Clean stickies with one idea each cluster beautifully. Stickies containing three thoughts, a name, and an arrow drawn on top get grouped badly — which describes most real workshop boards.
- **Handwriting and images are mostly invisible to it.** Anything drawn or pasted as a picture is not part of the synthesis, so hybrid in-person/remote sessions lose content.
- **It cannot facilitate.** The hard parts of a workshop — surfacing disagreement, cutting scope, deciding — are untouched, and no feature here pretends otherwise.

## Pricing

As of August 2026, Miro's structure is unchanged in shape: a free tier with a small board limit, then Starter and Business tiers priced per user per month (roughly the $8 and $16 range on annual billing), plus Enterprise. AI credits scale with the tier — free accounts get a token allowance, Business gets a substantially larger pool, and Enterprise negotiates.

Treat those numbers as approximate and check the current pricing page; per-seat tools shuffle their tiers often, and AI allowances have been the most frequently adjusted part of the plan for the last two years. The important budgeting question is not the seat price but whether your AI usage pattern fits the credit pool, because that is where teams get surprised.

## Who It's For

**Good fit:** facilitators and design leads who run recurring workshops, retros, or discovery sessions and are tired of the synthesis backlog. Product and ops teams that already standardize on Miro. Anyone who diagrams often enough to value a first draft but not often enough to master a dedicated diagramming tool.

**Poor fit:** teams that use Miro occasionally and would be upgrading purely for AI. Anyone who wants precise, spec-grade architecture diagrams — a dedicated tool with real layout control is still better. Solo users, who can get most of the value out of a general assistant and a lighter canvas.

## Verdict

Miro AI is a competent set of chores-automation features rather than a reinvention of the whiteboard, and that is the right ambition. Clustering and summarization remove the two tasks most likely to be skipped after a session, and diagram generation removes the blank canvas. None of it is impressive in isolation; together it makes Miro noticeably less annoying to use well.

The honest limitation is that it improves the *around* of collaborative work, not the work itself. A badly run workshop produces a badly clustered board very quickly. If your team already runs good sessions in Miro and just loses steam at the synthesis step, this is a real time-saver worth the tier. If the boards themselves are the problem, no amount of AI tidying will fix that.

**Rating: 3.5/5** — genuinely useful where it applies, limited by credits and by the bland quality of anything it writes.
