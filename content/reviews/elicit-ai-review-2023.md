Anyone who has started a literature review knows the grind: dozens of tabs, half-read abstracts, and a growing spreadsheet you keep forgetting to update. Elicit, built by the research lab Ought, aims squarely at that pain. It is an AI research assistant that helps you find papers, digest them, and organize what they actually say.

I put it through a few real research questions to see whether it earns a spot in a serious workflow or just adds another tab to the pile. Here is what stood out.

## What is Elicit?

Elicit is a web-based tool that uses language models to assist with academic-style research. You type a research question in plain English, and it searches a large database of papers, returns relevant results, and — this is the useful part — summarizes each one and can extract specific information into a structured table.

Rather than replacing the researcher, it tries to automate the tedious parts: scanning abstracts, spotting which papers are relevant, and lining up their key details side by side. It is best thought of as an assistant for the *discovery and triage* phase of research, not a tool that writes your paper for you.

## Key Features

### Question-based paper search

Instead of hunting for exact keywords, you ask a question the way you would ask a colleague — "does remote work affect team productivity?" Elicit interprets the intent and surfaces papers relevant to it. For exploratory work, where you do not yet know the right search terms, this is genuinely helpful.

### Abstract summaries

For each paper it returns, Elicit generates a short summary of the abstract in accessible language. When you are triaging thirty results, being able to read a one-line takeaway before committing to the full text saves a lot of time.

### Data extraction into tables

This is the standout feature. Elicit can pull specific fields — sample size, study population, intervention, main outcome — out of a set of papers and arrange them in a comparison table. Building that table by hand is one of the most tedious parts of a review, and having a first draft generated automatically is a real accelerator.

### Related-paper discovery

Beyond your initial results, Elicit helps you branch outward to related work, which is useful for making sure you have not missed an important cluster of research. It complements citation-chasing tools rather than fully replacing them.

## What I Like

1. **Cuts triage time** — Reading generated summaries before full abstracts genuinely speeds up the "is this relevant?" decision.
2. **The extraction table** — Automatically populating a comparison table across papers is the feature that makes Elicit worth opening.
3. **Natural-language search** — Asking a question instead of guessing keywords fits how research actually starts.
4. **Links back to sources** — Every result points to the real paper, so you can verify rather than trust blindly.
5. **Low barrier to try** — It is free right now and works in the browser, so there is little cost to experimenting.

## Cons and Limitations

1. **Summaries can mislead** — AI-generated summaries occasionally flatten or misstate a finding. You cannot cite the summary; you have to read the paper.
2. **Coverage gaps** — The paper database is broad but not exhaustive. For some niche or very recent topics, key work may be missing.
3. **Extraction needs checking** — The auto-filled table is a draft. Numbers and details need verification against the source before you rely on them.
4. **Not a writing tool** — Elicit helps you gather and organize; it does not synthesize an argument or draft prose. That is still on you.
5. **Evolving product** — It is clearly still maturing, and features and limits shift, so a workflow you build today may change.

## Pricing

As of August 2023, Elicit is **free to use**. The team has indicated that the tool is still being figured out commercially, and given that it depends on language models — which cost money to run — it is reasonable to expect usage limits or paid tiers down the line. Use it now, but do not build a mission-critical process around today's free access without watching for changes.

## Who It's For

**Great fit:**

- Graduate students and researchers starting a literature review
- Anyone doing evidence-based work who needs to scan many papers quickly
- Analysts and writers who need to ground claims in real studies
- People exploring an unfamiliar field who do not yet know the right search terms

**Less useful for:**

- Deep reading of a small, known set of papers you will read fully anyway
- Fields poorly covered by the underlying database
- Anyone expecting it to write or fully synthesize a review

## Verdict

**Rating: 4.2/5**

Elicit is one of the more genuinely useful AI research tools available right now, precisely because it aims at a real, boring problem — triaging and organizing papers — instead of trying to do everything. The abstract summaries and, especially, the data-extraction table can compress hours of scanning and copying into minutes.

The catch is the one that applies to every AI tool touching serious work: it is a first pass, not a final answer. Summaries can mislead, coverage has gaps, and extracted data needs checking against the source. Used with that discipline, Elicit is a strong accelerator for the discovery phase of research.

If you are a student or researcher facing a literature review — or you are a curious learner trying to get oriented in a field, much like the readers of our [AI tools for students](/ai-tools-for-students/) guide — Elicit is well worth an afternoon of experimentation. **Bottom line:** let it do the scanning and the table-building, then do the reading and the thinking yourself.
