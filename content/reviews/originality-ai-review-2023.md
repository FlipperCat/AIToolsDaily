ChatGPT has been public for about three months, and every editor who buys freelance content is now asking the same question: did a person write this, or a chatbot? Originality.ai is one of the first paid tools to make that question its entire business.

Where [GPTZero](/reviews/gptzero-review-2023-can-it-really-detect-chatgpt-writing/) took off with teachers, Originality.ai pitches itself at content marketers, SEO agencies, and website owners who pay writers by the word and want to know what they are buying. We ran it against a mix of human-written articles, raw ChatGPT output, and edited AI drafts to see how far you can trust it.

## What it is

Originality.ai is a web-based checker that scans text for two things at once: signs of AI generation and plagiarism from existing web pages. You paste text or a URL, it returns an "Original" versus "AI" score, and if you enable plagiarism checking, it lists matching sources.

The company says its model was trained to recognize output from GPT-3, GPT-3.5, and ChatGPT specifically. That focus makes sense today, since those models produce most of the AI copy that reaches an editor's inbox.

It is a paid tool from the first scan, with no free tier to speak of. That alone separates it from the free detectors that have appeared since January, including OpenAI's own AI Text Classifier.

## Key features

**AI detection score.** Each scan returns a percentage split between "Original" and "AI." The score is a confidence estimate for the whole text, not a sentence count. A 60% AI result means "probably AI, not certain." It does not mean 60% of the paragraphs were generated. Expect your team to misread this until someone explains it.

**Plagiarism checking.** You can run a traditional plagiarism scan in the same pass. For content buyers, this is the more established half of the product, since spun or copied articles were a problem long before ChatGPT.

**Chrome extension.** The extension lets you scan text from the page you are viewing, including drafts in Google Docs or a CMS, without copying and pasting into the dashboard.

**Team management.** You can add editors and writers, share a credit balance, and see who scanned what. Agencies reviewing dozens of articles a week will use this more than any other feature.

**Scan history.** Every result is saved, so you can go back to a disputed article and show a writer exactly what the tool reported and when.

**API access.** Developers can wire detection into a content pipeline, for example by scanning every submission before it reaches an editor's queue.

## How accurate is it?

This is the question that matters, so here is what we saw in hands-on testing. It is a small sample, not a formal benchmark.

- **Raw ChatGPT output:** Unedited answers to generic prompts ("write a 600-word blog post about home composting") were flagged as AI most of the time, often with very high confidence. On this kind of text, Originality.ai was more decisive than the free detectors we tried.
- **Lightly edited AI drafts:** After a human rewrote the intro, swapped some phrasing, and added a personal example, the scores dropped and became less consistent. Some edited drafts still came back strongly AI. Others landed in the murky middle.
- **Paraphrased AI text:** Running ChatGPT output through a paraphrasing tool lowered scores noticeably. The detector is not easy to fool, but it is not hard to fool either.
- **Human writing:** Most of our human samples scored as original. Not all. Formulaic writing, such as short how-to articles, product descriptions, and anything that reads like a template, occasionally came back with AI scores high enough to make an editor nervous.

That last point is the one to remember. False positives are rare enough that you might not notice them, and common enough that you will eventually accuse someone wrongly if you treat the score as a verdict.

Short text is also a problem. Below a few hundred words, results swing around. The tool works best on full articles, which is what it was built for.

## Pros

- **Built for content workflows.** Team seats, shared credits, scan history, and an API fit how agencies and publishers already work.
- **AI and plagiarism in one scan.** Fewer tools to juggle when you are reviewing freelance submissions.
- **Decisive on unedited AI text.** If a writer pasted ChatGPT output straight into your CMS, Originality.ai will very likely catch it.
- **Pay for what you use.** Credit-based pricing means a small site reviewing a few articles a month is not stuck with a large subscription.
- **Saved evidence.** Scan history gives you something concrete when you discuss a result with a writer.

## Cons and limitations

- **False positives exist.** Plain, formulaic human writing can score as AI. That is a serious problem if you act on scores alone.
- **Easy to misread.** The percentage looks like a measurement of how much text is AI. It is not, and that confusion causes bad decisions.
- **Edited AI text slips through.** Detection gets unreliable as soon as a human meaningfully revises a draft, which is exactly how many writers now use ChatGPT.
- **No real free tier.** You have to buy credits to evaluate it properly.
- **An arms race it cannot win permanently.** Every new language model and paraphrasing trick forces detectors to retrain. Today's accuracy is not guaranteed next quarter.
- **Not designed for classrooms.** The product, pricing, and messaging are aimed at publishers. Schools have different stakes and should be more cautious.

## Pricing (as of March 2023)

Originality.ai uses prepaid credits rather than a monthly plan. As of early 2023:

- Roughly **one credit scans 100 words** for AI detection.
- Credits work out to about **a penny per 100 words**, sold in packs. The entry pack was around $20 when we tested.
- Running plagiarism checks alongside AI detection uses additional credits.
- Credits are shared across a team account.

At those rates, scanning a 1,500-word article costs pennies, which is trivial next to what you paid the writer. Prices and packaging change often in this category, so check the current pricing page before you budget.

## Who it's for

**Good fit:**
- **Content agencies and SEO teams** buying articles from freelancers at volume.
- **Website owners and publishers** who want a screening step before content goes live.
- **Editors** who need a shared, auditable record of what was scanned.

**Poor fit:**
- **Teachers making misconduct decisions.** The false-positive risk is too high to use any detector as proof.
- **Writers checking their own work for "AI tells."** You will end up rewriting good sentences to satisfy a model.
- **Anyone wanting a free, quick check.** Free detectors exist, though they come with their own accuracy problems.

## How to use it responsibly

If you adopt Originality.ai, set a few house rules:

1. **Use the score as a trigger, not a verdict.** A high AI score means "look closer," not "reject."
2. **Ask for process evidence.** Outlines, drafts, and revision history in Google Docs tell you far more than a percentage.
3. **Talk to the writer.** Ask how they produced the piece. Many writers now use AI for outlines or research, and your policy should say what is allowed.
4. **Judge the content itself.** Generic, unsourced, fact-light writing is a problem whether a human or [ChatGPT](/reviews/chatgpt-review-2026-is-it-still-the-best-ai-tool/) produced it.
5. **Re-test periodically.** Detection quality shifts as new models arrive.

## Verdict

Originality.ai is the most practical AI detector we have tested for people who buy content professionally. The team features, scan history, plagiarism checks, and cheap per-word pricing fit agency and publisher workflows better than the free tools aimed at classrooms.

The detection itself is useful but limited. It reliably catches lazy, copy-pasted ChatGPT output. It struggles with edited AI text, and it sometimes flags real human writing. Use it as one input in an editorial process that also looks at drafts, sourcing, and quality. If you expect it to be a lie detector, you will make mistakes.

**Rating: 3.8/5.** A solid screening tool for content teams, as long as nobody treats the number as proof.
