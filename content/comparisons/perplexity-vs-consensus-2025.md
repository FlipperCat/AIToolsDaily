Both Perplexity and Consensus promise to replace hours of Googling with a cited answer in seconds — but they're aimed at different questions. Perplexity is a general-purpose answer engine over the live web, while Consensus is a search engine over peer-reviewed research papers. If you're trying to decide which one deserves your subscription in 2025, here's how they actually differ.

## At a Glance

| Dimension | Perplexity | Consensus |
|---|---|---|
| Best for | General research, current events, mixed questions | Evidence-based, scientific questions |
| Sources | Live web pages, news, plus academic mode | ~200M+ academic papers |
| Answer style | Conversational summary with citations | Study-by-study evidence with citations |
| Standout feature | Follow-up threads, Focus modes, Pro Search | Consensus Meter for yes/no questions |
| Freshness | Real-time web | As current as indexed research |
| Free tier | Yes, generous | Yes, limited searches |
| Paid price (approx.) | ~$20/month | ~$9–12/month |

## Sources and Coverage

This is the fundamental split. **Perplexity searches the open web** — news sites, blogs, documentation, forums — and synthesizes an answer with numbered citations you can click. It also offers an academic focus mode that narrows to scholarly sources when you want them. That breadth means Perplexity can answer almost anything: a product comparison, a coding question, a definition, or last week's headlines.

**Consensus searches academic literature**, drawing on a corpus of well over 200 million papers. Ask it a research question and it surfaces relevant studies, summarizes each one's finding, and links to the paper. It does not know about yesterday's news or a company's pricing page, and it isn't trying to — its entire value is grounding answers in published research rather than the general web.

The practical takeaway: if your question is "what does the evidence say about X," Consensus is purpose-built. If your question is "help me understand X, compare options, or find current information," Perplexity is far more flexible.

## Answer Quality and Format

**Perplexity** reads like a sharp assistant. You get a few paragraphs summarizing the answer, inline citations, and the ability to ask follow-up questions in a thread that keeps context. Pro Search runs a more thorough, multi-step process for harder queries, and you can steer which underlying model handles the reasoning. The output is designed to be *read as a conclusion*.

**Consensus** is deliberately more granular. Instead of one confident summary, it shows you a list of studies — each with a one-line takeaway — so you can see how the evidence stacks up. Its signature **Consensus Meter** tackles yes/no research questions (e.g., "does intermittent fasting aid weight loss?") by aggregating what the papers conclude into a quick at-a-glance verdict. The output is designed to be *interrogated as evidence*.

If you want a decision handed to you, Perplexity feels faster. If you want to see the underlying studies and judge for yourself, Consensus is more honest about scientific uncertainty.

## Citations and Trust

Both tools cite, which already puts them ahead of a plain chatbot. But the *nature* of the citations differs. Perplexity's sources are whatever the web offers — sometimes an authoritative journal, sometimes a marketing blog — so you have to weigh source quality yourself. Consensus links exclusively to academic papers, which raises the baseline credibility for research questions but narrows what it can address.

With either, the rule holds: **click through and verify**. AI summaries can misrepresent a source, and a citation appearing next to a claim isn't proof the source actually supports it. For an academic argument, Consensus makes verification easier because the paper is right there. For general questions, Perplexity's transparency about sources is one of its best features — a point we cover more in [Perplexity vs Google](/compare/perplexity-vs-google/).

## Features and Workflow

**Perplexity** wins on versatility. Focus modes let you scope a search to academic sources, video, or the general web. Threaded follow-ups make it feel like a research conversation, and file uploads let you ask questions about your own documents. It's the tool you keep open all day for whatever comes up.

**Consensus** wins on depth for one job. Beyond the meter, it offers study snapshots, filters for population or study type, and summaries tuned to scientific writing. It's the tool you open specifically when you need literature to back a claim — for a paper, a health decision, or a policy argument.

Neither replaces the other cleanly. Plenty of researchers run both: Consensus to gather the evidence base, Perplexity to explore around it and pull in context the literature doesn't cover.

## Pricing (as of April 2025)

Prices are approximate and change, so confirm current plans before subscribing.

- **Perplexity** — a capable free tier, with **Pro at roughly $20/month** unlocking unlimited Pro Search, file uploads, and model selection.
- **Consensus** — a **free plan with limited searches**, and **Premium around $9–12/month** for unlimited searches and full access to the meter and study tools.

For pure cost, Consensus is cheaper. But they're not really substitutes at the checkout — you're paying for different capabilities. Whether Perplexity's broader toolkit justifies the higher price is the crux of our [is Perplexity Pro worth it](/perplexity-pro-worth-it/) breakdown.

## Which Should You Choose?

**Choose Perplexity if** you want one flexible research tool for everything — current events, comparisons, coding, general learning — with the option to narrow to academic sources when needed. It's the better daily driver and the stronger pick for most people. If you want to automate repeatable research, our tutorial on [automating research with Perplexity](/tutorials/automate-research-with-perplexity/) shows how.

**Choose Consensus if** your work lives in evidence — students, researchers, clinicians, or anyone who needs to know what peer-reviewed studies actually say and wants citations straight to the papers. Its narrow focus is a feature, not a limitation.

**Choose both if** you do serious research regularly. Use Consensus to build the evidence foundation and Perplexity to fill in everything the literature can't. Together they cost less than many single premium subscriptions, and they cover complementary halves of the research process. Pick based on whether your hardest questions are about *the web* or about *the evidence* — and if they're both, there's no shame in paying for the pair.
