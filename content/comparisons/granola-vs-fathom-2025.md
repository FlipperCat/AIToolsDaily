Most AI meeting tools are the same product with different logos: a bot joins your call, transcribes it, and emails you a summary. Granola and Fathom both do meeting notes, but they disagree on something fundamental — whether a robot should visibly join your meeting at all.

That single architectural choice cascades into everything else: what they can record, who sees that you're using them, what they cost, and who they're actually for. Here's how they compare after running both across a couple of months of real calls.

## Quick Comparison

| | Granola | Fathom |
|---|---|---|
| **Approach** | Silent desktop app, captures system audio | Bot joins the meeting |
| **Visible to others?** | No | Yes — appears in participant list |
| **Platforms** | macOS desktop | Mac, Windows, web |
| **Meeting support** | Anything that makes sound, including in-person | Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams |
| **Video recording** | No — audio and notes only | Yes, with playback |
| **Your own notes** | Core to the product — AI enhances what you type | Optional highlights during the call |
| **Free tier** | Limited number of meetings | Generous — unlimited recording and transcription |
| **Paid pricing (approx., Apr 2025)** | ~$18/user/month | ~$19/user/month, team tier around $29 |
| **CRM integration** | Limited | Strong (HubSpot, Salesforce, Close) |
| **Best for** | Founders, consultants, anyone in back-to-back calls | Sales teams, anyone who needs the recording |

## How They Actually Work

**Granola** installs as a Mac app and listens to your computer's audio output and microphone. Nothing joins the call. You open a notes pane and type whatever sparse fragments you'd normally scribble — "pricing objection," "follow up re: Q3," a half-sentence you'd never understand later. When the meeting ends, Granola merges your fragments with the full transcript and produces notes that read like you wrote them properly.

That last bit is the actual product insight. Granola isn't trying to replace your notes; it's trying to make bad notes good. The output is shaped by what *you* cared about, which is why it tends to beat generic AI summaries that weight every topic equally.

**Fathom** takes the standard approach: connect your calendar, and a bot named after the product joins your Zoom, Meet, or Teams calls. It records video and audio, transcribes with speaker labels, and generates summaries, action items, and shareable clips. You can tap a highlight button mid-call to mark moments worth revisiting.

## Note Quality

Granola wins on notes that reflect the meeting *you* had. Because your typed fragments anchor the summary, the output emphasizes the pricing objection you flagged rather than the ten minutes of small talk. Its cross-meeting chat — ask a question across your whole meeting history — is genuinely useful once you've accumulated a few weeks of calls.

Fathom's summaries are strong on structure. Action items with owners, topic sections, and next steps come out clean and consistently formatted, which matters more than prose quality when the notes are going to a team. Its "ask" feature over an individual call is solid for "what did they say about the contract term?"

The honest verdict: Granola produces better notes for the person who was in the meeting. Fathom produces better notes for everyone who wasn't.

## The Bot Question

This is where you should actually make your decision.

**Granola's invisibility is a feature.** No participant list awkwardness, no "do you mind if I record this?" derailing the first minute, no external guests declining the bot. It also records in-person conversations, phone calls on speaker, and any platform at all — because it's just capturing audio, not integrating with a meeting API.

It's also an ethical and legal consideration you have to handle yourself. Recording consent laws vary by jurisdiction, and a tool that no one can see is a tool that no one knows to object to. Granola's design assumes you'll tell people. Do that.

**Fathom's visibility is also a feature.** The bot in the participant list *is* the disclosure. In regulated industries, on customer calls, or in any organization with a recording policy, that transparency is the whole point — and being able to say "the notetaker is in the room" is often easier than a verbal disclaimer.

## Coverage and Platform Limits

Granola was Mac-only as of April 2025. If your team is on Windows, the comparison ends here. It also captures audio only — there's no video recording to rewatch, which matters if you review demos or user-testing sessions.

Fathom covers Mac, Windows, and web, and records video. But it only works on the three major meeting platforms it integrates with. In-person meetings, phone calls, and niche conferencing tools are out of scope. If your calls are all on Zoom, this is irrelevant; if half your important conversations happen in a room or on a phone, it's disqualifying.

## Sharing and Team Workflows

Fathom is built for distribution. Recordings get shared with timestamped links, clips get pulled out and dropped in Slack, and call data syncs into a CRM so the account record updates without anyone typing. For a sales team, that pipeline is the product — the notes are almost a byproduct. It's the same territory covered in our [Fathom vs Otter comparison](/compare/fathom-vs-otter-2024/) and by [tl;dv](/reviews/tldv-review-2025/), which chases the same sales-and-research audience.

Granola started as a single-player tool and grew shared spaces later. As of early 2025 you can share notes and organize them into folders for a team, but it's lighter than Fathom on integrations and there's no video artifact to circulate. Granola's team story is "we all read the same notes," not "the CRM updates itself."

## Pricing

Approximate, as of April 2025 — check current pricing before committing.

**Granola** gives you a set number of free meetings to evaluate, then runs around $18/user/month, with business plans for teams. There's no meaningful free-forever tier; it expects to be paid for.

**Fathom's** free plan is unusually generous — unlimited recording and transcription with AI summaries, which is more than most competitors give away. Paid tiers around $19/user/month unlock unlimited AI features, better search, and integrations, with a team tier near $29/user/month adding CRM sync and admin controls.

For solo users, Fathom's free tier is hard to argue with. For teams, the two land close enough on price that features, not cost, should decide it.

## Where Both Fall Short

Neither fixes bad audio. Crosstalk, heavy accents, and laptop mics degrade transcripts in both, and every AI summary inherits the transcript's mistakes.

Both also encourage a subtle bad habit: outsourcing attention. Knowing something is capturing everything makes it easier to half-listen. Granola pushes back a little by requiring you to type *something*; Fathom doesn't push back at all.

And both produce summaries that read as slightly generic. Skim before forwarding — an AI-generated action item assigned to the wrong person is worse than no action item.

## Which Should You Choose?

**Choose Granola if** you're a founder, consultant, recruiter, or anyone whose day is back-to-back calls where *you* need to remember the substance. You're on a Mac, you take notes anyway, you'd rather not have a bot announce itself to a prospect, and some of your important conversations happen in person or on the phone.

**Choose Fathom if** you need the recording as an artifact — sales calls to review, demos to clip, interviews to share with a hiring panel. You're on Windows, or your team is mixed. You want CRM sync. Or you simply want a capable meeting recorder for free, which Fathom uniquely offers.

**Choose neither if** you need deep search across a large organization's meeting archive with enterprise admin controls; that's where the older incumbents like [Otter](/reviews/16-otter-ai-review/) still have an edge, and our [roundup of AI meeting assistants](/best-ai-meeting-assistants/) covers the wider field.

The instinct to pick based on summary quality is the wrong instinct — they're close. Pick based on whether you need a visible recording or an invisible memory.
