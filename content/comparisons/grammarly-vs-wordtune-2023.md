Grammarly and Wordtune both sit in your browser and offer suggestions as you type, so they often get lumped together. In practice they solve different problems. **Grammarly** is built to find what's *wrong* with your writing. **Wordtune**, from the Israeli AI lab AI21 Labs, is built to show you other ways to *say* it.

We used both side by side for several weeks on emails, blog drafts, LinkedIn posts, and a few reports. Here's how they compare and which one is worth your money.

## Quick comparison

| | Grammarly | Wordtune |
|---|---|---|
| Core strength | Grammar, spelling, clarity, and tone checks | Rewriting sentences in alternative phrasings |
| How it works | Underlines issues as you type | Suggests rewrites for a sentence you select |
| Free plan | Spelling, grammar, punctuation, basic tone | A limited number of rewrites per day |
| Paid plan (approx., as of Feb 2023) | ~$12/mo billed annually, ~$30 month-to-month | ~$10/mo billed annually, ~$25 month-to-month |
| Tone controls | Tone detector, formality and goal settings | Casual / formal rewrite modes |
| Length controls | Conciseness suggestions | Shorten / expand modes |
| Plagiarism check | Yes (Premium) | No |
| Summarization | No | Yes, via Wordtune Read |
| Desktop apps | Windows and Mac apps, Office add-in | Word add-in on Windows, web editor |
| Team features | Business plan with style guides and analytics | Limited |
| Best for | Error-free, polished writing | Finding better phrasing |

## Grammar and spelling: Grammarly wins clearly

This is Grammarly's home turf. It catches spelling mistakes, subject-verb agreement, missing articles, comma splices, and misused words with very few false alarms in normal business writing. Premium adds deeper checks for passive voice, wordiness, vague language, and inconsistent formatting.

Wordtune isn't designed as a proofreader. Its rewrites are usually grammatical, and they sometimes fix errors along the way, but it won't systematically scan your document and flag every mistake. If you paste a sloppy draft into Wordtune alone, mistakes in sentences you didn't select will go unnoticed.

**Verdict:** If your main goal is fewer errors, Grammarly is the right tool. It isn't close.

## Rewriting and phrasing: Wordtune's specialty

Select a sentence in Wordtune and you get several alternative versions. You can nudge them with modes:

- **Rewrite:** general alternatives with the same meaning
- **Casual / Formal:** shift the register
- **Shorten / Expand:** tighten a rambling sentence or add detail to a thin one

The suggestions are genuinely varied. They restructure the sentence rather than swapping in synonyms. When you know a sentence is clunky but can't see why, cycling through three or four Wordtune options is often faster than rewriting it yourself.

Grammarly Premium does offer full-sentence rewrites for clarity, but they're reactive: Grammarly suggests them when it detects a problem, and you usually get one suggestion rather than a menu. It's editing, not brainstorming.

**Verdict:** For phrasing, especially in emails and social posts where voice matters, Wordtune is more useful.

## Tone and audience

Grammarly's **tone detector** reads your text and tells you how it's likely to come across: confident, friendly, worried, and so on. Premium users can set **goals** for audience, formality, domain, and intent, and suggestions adjust to match. That's valuable for a tense email to a client where you want to catch unintended defensiveness before hitting send.

Wordtune handles tone by *changing* it: take a sentence and make it more formal or more casual. It doesn't analyze the overall tone of a message.

The two approaches complement each other. Grammarly tells you there's a problem, and Wordtune gives you options to fix it.

## Integrations and where they work

**Grammarly** is everywhere. Its browser extension covers Gmail, Google Docs, LinkedIn, and most web text boxes. Desktop apps for Windows and Mac bring suggestions into native apps, and there's a Microsoft Word and Outlook add-in plus a mobile keyboard. If you write in a lot of different places, this breadth is a major advantage.

**Wordtune** works mainly through its Chrome extension, which covers most web text fields, plus its own web editor and a Word add-in on Windows. Coverage is good for browser-based work but thinner outside the browser.

One practical note: running both extensions at once works, but the overlapping pop-ups can get crowded. Some writers keep Grammarly always on and turn Wordtune on only while drafting.

## Extras: plagiarism vs summaries

Grammarly Premium includes a **plagiarism checker** that compares your text against web pages and academic databases. For students and content marketers checking freelance submissions, that alone can justify the upgrade.

Wordtune has **Wordtune Read**, which summarizes long articles and PDFs into key points. It's handy for research, though it's a separate workflow from writing.

## Privacy and data

Both tools process your text on their servers, so both require some trust. Grammarly offers enterprise-grade controls on its Business plan and publishes security documentation aimed at IT teams. Wordtune is newer to the enterprise market. If you're writing under NDA or in a regulated industry, check your company's policy before installing either extension. They read what you type in supported fields.

## Pricing (approximate, as of February 2023)

**Grammarly:**
- **Free:** core spelling, grammar, and punctuation checks plus tone detection
- **Premium:** about $12/month billed annually, or around $30 month-to-month
- **Business:** around $15 per member per month billed annually, with a team minimum, adding shared style guides and admin analytics

**Wordtune:**
- **Free:** a small daily allowance of rewrites and summaries
- **Premium:** roughly $10/month billed annually, or about $25 month-to-month, with unlimited rewrites and summaries
- **Business/team options:** available but less developed than Grammarly's

Both companies run discounts frequently, so check current pricing before you buy. Wordtune's free tier is also generous enough to show you whether rewrite suggestions fit how you write.

## Non-native English speakers

This is where the choice gets interesting. Many non-native speakers find **Wordtune** more helpful day to day. The problem often isn't grammar errors but sentences that are technically correct yet sound unnatural. Seeing several native-sounding alternatives teaches phrasing in a way error underlines don't.

Grammarly still matters for this group, though, because it catches the article and preposition errors that are hardest to self-edit. If budget allows one tool, pick based on which problem you hit more: mistakes (Grammarly) or awkward phrasing (Wordtune). ## Which should you choose?

**Choose Grammarly if:**
- You need reliable proofreading across email, docs, and desktop apps
- You're a student or content manager who wants the plagiarism checker
- You manage a team and need shared style guides
- You write high-stakes messages where tone slips are costly

**Choose Wordtune if:**
- Your writing is already mostly correct but sounds flat or clunky
- You write lots of short-form content like emails, LinkedIn posts, and cold outreach
- You're a non-native speaker who wants more natural phrasing
- You want quick summaries of long articles for research

**Use both if** you write for a living. Grammarly's free plan plus Wordtune Premium is a strong, affordable setup: free error-catching and paid phrasing help.
