Grammarly is the grammar checker most people have heard of. LanguageTool is the one people find when they write in more than one language, want something cheaper, or care where their text goes. Both catch typos and clumsy sentences, but they are built for different writers.

We used both for several weeks across emails, blog drafts, documentation, and some German and Spanish text, using the browser extensions and the desktop apps. Here is how they compare.

## Quick comparison

| | Grammarly | LanguageTool |
|---|---|---|
| **Languages** | English only (US, UK, Canadian, Australian, Indian variants) | 30+ languages, with the deepest support in English, German, Spanish, French, Portuguese, and Dutch |
| **Free plan** | Core spelling, grammar, punctuation, and a tone check | Spelling and grammar with daily and length limits on some features |
| **Generative AI** | GrammarlyGO for drafting and rewriting, with monthly prompt allowances | AI rephrasing for sentences, more limited in scope |
| **Style and clarity** | Strong: clarity rewrites, conciseness, tone suggestions | Good, with an optional "Picky" mode for stricter style checks |
| **Plagiarism check** | Included in Premium | Not a core feature |
| **Self-hosting** | No | Yes, via the open-source core |
| **Integrations** | Browser, desktop app, Word, Google Docs, mobile keyboard | Browser, desktop app, Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice, plus community plugins for editors like VS Code and Obsidian |
| **Approx. price (Jan 2024)** | Premium around $12/month billed annually, about $30 month-to-month | Premium often around half of Grammarly's price or less on annual billing |
| **Best for** | Native or near-native English writers who want polish | Multilingual writers, privacy-conscious users, and budget buyers |

Prices are approximate as of January 2024 and change often, especially with promotions. Check both pricing pages before buying.

## Accuracy on everyday English

For basic spelling, grammar, and punctuation, the two are closer than the price gap suggests. Both caught every obvious typo we planted, agreement errors, and most comma problems.

Grammarly pulls ahead on **clarity and style**. It's better at spotting wordy sentences, passive voice that hides who did what, and phrases that make a paragraph harder to read, and it gives cleaner rewrite suggestions. If your English is already correct and you want it to read better, Grammarly is the stronger editor.

LanguageTool is a little more literal. It flags real errors reliably but suggests fewer stylistic rewrites by default. Turning on Picky mode adds more style checks (redundancy, filler words, long sentences), which closes some of the gap, along with more false positives.

Both still make mistakes. Both occasionally "correct" technical terms, product names, and deliberate stylistic choices. Add your own terms to the personal dictionary early.

## Languages: LanguageTool's big advantage

This is the deciding factor for many people. Grammarly works only in English. If you write in German, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch, or a dozen other languages, Grammarly can't check that text.

LanguageTool can, and it detects the language automatically as you type, so you can switch between an English email and a Spanish one without changing settings. Quality varies: the major European languages are well covered, while smaller languages get fewer and simpler checks. In our German and Spanish tests, it caught most errors a careful native proofreader would want fixed.

For non-native English writers who also write in their first language, LanguageTool's single tool for everything is a real advantage. If you need help with meaning rather than grammar, a dedicated tool like [DeepL Write](/reviews/deepl-write-review-2023-can-the-translation-giant-fix-your-writing/) is worth adding.

## AI rewriting

Grammarly launched GrammarlyGO in 2023, adding generative AI that can draft replies, shorten or expand paragraphs, change tone, and rewrite text based on a prompt. It works inside the same extension, so you don't need to switch to a separate chatbot. Free and paid plans include different monthly prompt allowances.

LanguageTool's AI features are narrower. It focuses on rephrasing individual sentences to be more formal, simpler, shorter, or more fluent, rather than generating full drafts. For many people that's enough. If you want an assistant to write a first draft, Grammarly does more, though a general chatbot or a dedicated paraphraser like [QuillBot](/reviews/quillbot-review-2023-is-the-paraphrasing-tool-worth-paying-for/) may be more flexible than either.

## Privacy and data control

Both companies say they don't sell your text and both encrypt data in transit. The difference is structural.

- **Grammarly** processes text in its cloud. It offers enterprise security features and admin controls for business plans, but there is no self-hosted option.
- **LanguageTool** is based in Germany and operates under GDPR. More importantly, its core engine is open source. Technical teams can run a LanguageTool server on their own machine or network and point the browser extension or editor plugins at it, so text never leaves their environment. The self-hosted version doesn't include all Premium rules, but it covers a lot.

For legal, healthcare, or internal engineering writing where sending text to a third party is a problem, that option alone can settle the decision.

## Integrations and everyday use

Grammarly's integrations are more polished. The browser extension works in most web text fields, the desktop app checks text in many native apps, and the mobile keyboard is good for writing on the go. The suggestion cards are clear and quick to act on.

LanguageTool covers the same main places (browser, desktop, Word, Google Docs) and adds LibreOffice, which Grammarly doesn't support. Its open-source ecosystem also has community plugins for code editors and note apps. This is useful for developers writing docs in Markdown, though setup takes more effort.

In day-to-day use, Grammarly felt slightly faster and less intrusive. LanguageTool occasionally showed underlines later or doubled up with built-in browser spell check until we turned the latter off.

## Pricing and value

As of January 2024, approximately:

- **Grammarly Premium** costs about $12 per month on an annual plan and about $30 on a monthly plan. Business plans are priced per member.
- **LanguageTool Premium** is usually much cheaper, often around half of Grammarly's price or less on annual billing, with team plans available.

For an English-only writer who uses clarity suggestions and GrammarlyGO every day, Grammarly's price can be justified. For anyone who writes in multiple languages or mainly needs reliable error-catching, LanguageTool is the better value.

Both free plans are worth trying first. Many people find a free plan covers 80% of what they need.

## Which should you choose?

**Choose Grammarly if:**

- You write mostly or only in English
- You want strong clarity and tone suggestions, not just error checks
- You'd use built-in generative AI for drafting and rewriting
- You want the most polished extension and mobile keyboard

**Choose LanguageTool if:**

- You write in more than one language
- Privacy matters, or you'd like the option to self-host
- You use LibreOffice, Markdown editors, or other tools Grammarly doesn't reach
- You want solid grammar checking for less money

**Consider something else if:** you want deeper long-form style analysis for books or reports (ProWritingAid is built for that), or you mainly want help rephrasing sentences rather than fixing errors (see [Grammarly vs Wordtune](/compare/grammarly-vs-wordtune-2023-error-checker-or-rewriting-partner/)).

## The bottom line

Grammarly is the better English writing coach. LanguageTool is the better multilingual, privacy-friendly checker. They aren't competing for exactly the same user, so the right choice usually comes down to which languages you write in and how much you care about where your text goes.
