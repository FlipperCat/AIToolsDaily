Microsoft Designer has been easy to try since Microsoft dropped the waitlist this spring, and it's openly aimed at the same people Canva has served for years: small business owners, marketers, and anyone who needs a decent social graphic without hiring a designer. Canva, meanwhile, spent the first half of 2023 adding its own AI features on top of a template library most competitors can't touch.

So which one should you use? We spent several weeks making the same kinds of assets in both: Instagram posts, event invitations, a simple promo flyer, and some blog headers. Short version: Designer is better at starting a design from a sentence, and Canva is better at everything after that. The details below explain where each one helps and where it gets in the way.

If you want the deeper solo verdict on Microsoft's tool, see our [Microsoft Designer review](/reviews/microsoft-designer-review-2023/).

## Quick Comparison

| | Canva | Microsoft Designer |
|---|---|---|
| **Status** | Mature product, AI features rolling out | Public preview |
| **Price (as of July 2023)** | Free plan; Pro roughly $120/year or $13/month for one person | Free during preview with a Microsoft account |
| **Starting point** | Templates, or Magic Design from an uploaded image | A text description of the design you want |
| **AI image generation** | Text to Image app (quality varies) | Built in, using OpenAI's DALL·E |
| **AI writing** | Magic Write (limited free uses) | Suggests headline and caption text from your prompt |
| **Editing control** | Deep: layers, grids, alignment, animation | Basic, improving with each update |
| **Templates** | Very large library | Small, AI-generated layouts instead |
| **Brand kit / teams** | Yes (Pro and Teams) | Not really yet |
| **Formats** | Social, print, video, docs, presentations, websites | Mostly social posts and simple graphics |
| **Best for** | Ongoing marketing work, teams, brand consistency | Fast one-off graphics from a prompt |

## Getting Started: Prompt vs Template

**Microsoft Designer** opens with a prompt box. Type something like "Instagram post announcing a Saturday farmers market, warm autumn colors, fresh produce," and within a few seconds you get several complete layouts with images, headline text, and a sensible arrangement. You can also drop in your own photos and Designer will build layouts around them.

For someone who freezes at a blank canvas, that first-minute experience is the best thing about Designer. It feels like describing what you want to a designer and getting rough drafts back.

**Canva** opens with templates, as it always has. You search "farmers market Instagram post," browse dozens or hundreds of options, and customize one. Canva's newer Magic Design feature works differently from Designer's prompt: you upload an image, pick a style, and Canva suggests templates built around it. It's useful, but it's closer to "smart template matching" than "generate a design from a sentence."

**Winner: Microsoft Designer** for speed from zero, as long as one of its first few options is close to what you wanted.

## Editing and Control

This is where the gap is widest.

**Canva** gives you the full toolkit: precise alignment, grouping, layers, transparency, grids, animation, resizing for multiple formats (resizing is a Pro feature), and a huge element library. If a template is 80% right, you can almost always fix the other 20% without fighting the tool.

**Microsoft Designer** is a preview product, and its editor shows it. You can change text, swap images, adjust colors, and move elements, but fine control is limited. Several times we liked a layout but couldn't make one specific change, like tightening text spacing or nudging an element exactly where we wanted it, without regenerating or starting over. Microsoft has been shipping editing improvements quickly during the preview, so expect this to change.

**Winner: Canva**, clearly. If you care about getting a design exactly right, Designer will frustrate you today.

## AI Image Quality

**Microsoft Designer** generates images with OpenAI's DALL·E, the same model family behind Bing Image Creator. Results are generally good for illustrative social graphics: food, landscapes, abstract backgrounds, product-style scenes. Like most image models right now, it struggles with hands, legible text inside the image, and anything requiring precise detail. Designer works around that by overlaying real, editable text on top of the generated image, which is the right call.

**Canva's** Text to Image app works, but in our tests its output was less consistent and needed more retries to get something usable. Canva also has Magic Edit (describe a change to part of an image) and Magic Eraser for removing objects, which are handy for cleaning up your own photos.

Neither matches dedicated image generators. If image quality is the priority, generate images elsewhere and bring them in. Our [Midjourney vs Bing Image Creator comparison](/compare/midjourney-vs-bing-image-creator-2023/) covers those options.

**Winner: Microsoft Designer** for generated images. **Canva** for editing photos you already have.

## Templates and Range of Formats

Canva's library is its moat. Social posts, stories, flyers, posters, business cards, presentations, documents, short videos, simple websites. If you need it, there's a template. That breadth matters more than any single AI feature when you're producing content every week.

Designer focuses on social posts, invitations, and simple graphics. It doesn't try to be a presentation tool or a video editor, and it doesn't have a template library in the traditional sense, since every layout is generated.

**Winner: Canva.**

## Brand Consistency and Teams

If you post for a business, consistency matters: same fonts, same colors, same logo placement. Canva Pro's Brand Kit stores those and makes them one click away, and Canva Teams adds shared folders, comments, and approval workflows.

Designer has no comparable brand tools or team collaboration in the preview. Every design starts fresh, which works for one-off graphics but becomes tedious for a business posting several times a week.

**Winner: Canva.**

## Pricing (as of July 2023)

- **Canva Free:** generous, with most templates and basic editing. Some AI tools are limited (Magic Write has a capped number of free uses) and some features, like Background Remover, Magic Eraser, and one-click resizing, are Pro-only.
- **Canva Pro:** roughly $120/year or about $13/month for one person, in the US.
- **Canva Teams:** priced per person; check current rates.
- **Microsoft Designer:** free during the preview with a Microsoft account. Microsoft has indicated premium features will eventually be tied to Microsoft 365 subscriptions, so don't assume it stays fully free.

Prices change and vary by region, so confirm before you buy. For a broader look at Canva's competition on price and features, see our [Canva vs Adobe Express comparison](/compare/canva-vs-adobe-express/).

## Privacy and Commercial Use

Both tools let you use what you create for business, but read the terms for AI-generated images specifically, since the legal picture around AI imagery is still unsettled. Designer is a preview, and preview terms can change. For anything high-stakes, such as a logo or a printed product, we'd avoid relying entirely on AI-generated imagery from either tool.

## Which Should You Choose?

**Choose Microsoft Designer if:**
- You make occasional one-off graphics and hate starting from a blank page
- You want AI-generated images built into the layout
- You don't need precise control or brand consistency
- You want something free to experiment with right now

**Choose Canva if:**
- You create marketing content regularly
- You need brand kits, team collaboration, or many formats beyond social posts
- You want precise control over the final design
- You're willing to pay for Pro, or you're happy with a strong free plan

**Our pick for most people: Canva.** Designer's prompt-to-design workflow is a better starting experience, and it's a good sign of where design tools are heading. But a design tool is mostly about the hundred small edits after the first draft, and Canva is far ahead there.

**A practical combo:** use Designer to brainstorm layouts and generate images, then rebuild the version you like in Canva for final polish and brand consistency. It costs nothing extra, and you get the best part of each tool.

We'll revisit this as Designer leaves preview. If Microsoft closes the editing gap while keeping the prompt-first workflow, this comparison could look very different in a year.
