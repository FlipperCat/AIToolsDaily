Meeting recorders have quietly become the most-used AI tool in a lot of companies — more than image generators, more than coding assistants. The bot joins, the transcript appears, someone searches it two weeks later. Fathom and tl;dv are two of the strongest options in that category, and they're close enough that most comparisons end in a shrug.

They aren't interchangeable, though. Fathom is built around *the call you just had* — fast, clean summaries you can send five minutes after hanging up. tl;dv is built around *the calls you've had over time* — clips, playlists, and cross-meeting search that suits research and sales-coaching workflows. Pick the wrong one and you'll spend months fighting the tool's default assumptions.

Here's the practical breakdown.

## Quick Comparison

| Dimension | Fathom | tl;dv |
|---|---|---|
| Core philosophy | Fast, polished summary of each call | Library of calls you mine over time |
| Free tier | Generous; recording + AI summaries | Generous; unlimited recording/transcription |
| Summary quality | Excellent, action-item heavy | Very good, more structured/templated |
| Clips & highlights | Live highlight button during calls | Strong clipping + reels from many calls |
| CRM sync | Solid, sales-oriented | Solid, with playbook/scorecard angle |
| Cross-meeting analysis | Basic | A core feature |
| Languages | Broad support | Broad support, notably strong here |
| Best for | Founders, consultants, call-heavy solos | Sales teams, UX researchers, managers |

## Summary Quality

This is the thing you'll judge them on every single day, and Fathom holds a small but real edge in *readability*. Its summaries tend to come out as something you could paste into Slack unedited: a short recap, decisions, and action items with owners attached. It's opinionated about structure, and the opinion is a good one.

tl;dv's summaries are more configurable. You can push it toward templates — discovery call, user interview, standup — and get consistent sections back. That's more work up front and more payoff if ten people need the same shape of notes every week. Out of the box, though, tl;dv's default output reads slightly more like a transcript digest and slightly less like a memo.

Both still miss things. Neither reliably catches a decision that was made implicitly ("yeah, let's just do that") without an explicit restatement. If a decision matters, say it out loud in a full sentence — that's true of every tool in this category, including the ones covered in our [best AI meeting assistants](/best-ai-meeting-assistants/) roundup.

## Recording and Coverage

Both join Zoom, Google Meet, and Microsoft Teams as a bot, and both offer a desktop capture path for the times a bot isn't welcome. Fathom's desktop app is the smoother of the two in practice — fewer "waiting to be admitted" moments.

tl;dv wins on scale. If you record dozens of calls a week and want them all retained and searchable, its free plan is unusually permissive about recording and transcription volume, gating features rather than minutes. Fathom's free tier is also generous but historically limits how many calls keep their full AI summary treatment.

Language coverage is a genuine tl;dv strength. If your team runs customer calls in several European languages, test it first — transcription and summarization hold up better than you'd expect from a mid-size tool.

## Clips, Highlights, and Reuse

Fathom's highlight button is the best-designed small feature in either product: tap it during the call, and you get a timestamped clip afterward without breaking your attention. That's ideal for solo operators who want three moments from a call, not a research corpus.

tl;dv goes further. You can tag moments across many meetings, then assemble reels — every objection about pricing from the last quarter, every time a user got stuck on onboarding. For a UX researcher or a sales manager building coaching material, that's a different category of usefulness, not just a nicer version of the same thing.

If your endgame is turning calls into published content rather than internal artifacts, neither tool is really the answer — a dedicated clipping workflow like the one in our guide to [turning long videos into shorts](/tutorials/turn-long-videos-into-shorts-ai/) will serve you better.

## CRM and Integrations

Both push notes, summaries, and call data into HubSpot and Salesforce on paid plans, and both connect to Slack, Notion, and the usual automation layers. Wiring either into a broader stack via [Zapier](/reviews/zapier-review/) or a self-hosted runner is straightforward.

The difference is emphasis. Fathom's CRM story is "get the notes into the record, automatically, without a rep touching anything." tl;dv's is "score the call against a playbook and show the manager where reps are drifting." If nobody at your company is coaching sales calls, that second layer is weight you're paying for and not using.

## Privacy and Consent

Worth more attention than it usually gets. Both record, both store transcripts in the cloud, both let admins configure retention on business plans. Neither absolves you of consent obligations — recording rules vary by jurisdiction, and a bot named after your company sitting in a call is not the same thing as documented consent.

Two practical habits regardless of tool: turn off automatic recording for calls involving HR, legal, or candidate conversations, and check whether your plan allows opting out of data being used for model improvement. Both vendors offer that control at the business level; the defaults on lower tiers deserve a read.

## Pricing

Approximate as of July 2026, and both vendors change tiers often — verify before buying:

- **Fathom**: free plan with recording and AI summaries; paid individual plans commonly land in the mid-teens to ~$30/user/month, with team plans above that for CRM sync and admin controls.
- **tl;dv**: free plan with unlimited recording and transcription; paid tiers typically start around $20/user/month and rise for CRM integrations, playbooks, and analytics.

For a solo user, both free tiers are legitimately usable for months. The paid decision usually gets forced by CRM sync or retention, not by summary quality.

## Where Each One Falls Down

**Fathom's limits.** Cross-meeting analysis is thin. If your question is "what did the last 30 customers say about onboarding," you'll be doing that manually. Template control over summary format is limited compared to tl;dv, and heavy-volume teams may find the library harder to navigate as it grows.

**tl;dv's limits.** More surface area means more setup. Teams that adopt it casually often end up using 20% of it and wondering why they're paying more than Fathom costs. Default summaries need tuning to feel as sharp. And like every bot-based recorder, it occasionally fails to join a call and you find out afterward.

**Both.** Speaker attribution degrades with crosstalk. Action items get invented occasionally — a suggestion that someone floated becomes a task with an owner. Skim before you forward.

## Which Should You Choose?

**Choose Fathom if** you're a founder, consultant, or account executive who wants the best per-call output with essentially zero configuration. It's the better default for individuals and small teams, and its free tier makes trialing painless.

**Choose tl;dv if** you're a sales manager building coaching loops, a UX researcher running interview batches, or a team that needs consistent templated notes across many people. The cross-meeting layer is the reason to pay, and if you won't use it, you're buying the wrong tool.
