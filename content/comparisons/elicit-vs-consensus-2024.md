Elicit and Consensus are the two names that come up whenever someone asks how to use AI for academic literature without the hallucination problem of general chatbots. Both search real, published papers and ground every claim in a citation. But after using both across several research projects, we think they're less interchangeable than they look: one is a workbench, the other is an answer engine. Here's the breakdown as of mid-2024.

## At a glance

| | Elicit | Consensus |
|---|---|---|
| **Core job** | Find papers and extract structured data from them | Answer a question with the balance of evidence |
| **Signature feature** | Data-extraction tables across many papers | Consensus Meter (% of studies saying yes/no) |
| **Corpus** | ~125M+ papers via Semantic Scholar | ~200M+ papers via Semantic Scholar |
| **PDF upload** | Yes — extract from your own PDFs | No |
| **Study-quality signals** | Extracted fields you choose (sample size, methods) | Labels like RCT, sample size, citation count |
| **Export** | CSV/BIB export of results tables | Limited (citations, shareable results) |
| **Free tier** | One-time credit allowance | Limited AI analyses per month |
| **Paid price (approx., mid-2024)** | ~$10–12/month | ~$9–12/month |
| **Best for** | Literature reviews, systematic reviews | Quick evidence checks, exploratory questions |

Pricing above is approximate as of July 2024 — both companies adjust tiers and credit systems frequently.

## How each one works

**Elicit** starts from a research question, returns a ranked table of relevant papers, and then lets you add columns: sample size, population, methodology, main findings, limitations. It reads each paper and fills in the cells with citations back to the source text. You can upload your own PDFs and run the same extraction across them. The workflow feels like a spreadsheet that reads papers for you — which is exactly what a literature review needs. For background on the tool itself, see our [Elicit review](/reviews/elicit-ai-review-2023/).

**Consensus** works like a search engine with an opinion meter. Ask "does creatine improve cognitive function?" and it returns relevant studies, short AI summaries of each, and — for yes/no questions — its Consensus Meter showing what share of the retrieved studies lean yes, no, or mixed. Quality labels (randomized controlled trial, large sample, highly cited) sit right on the results. Our [Consensus review](/reviews/consensus-ai-review-2023/) covers the mechanics in more depth.

## Search quality and coverage

Both draw primarily on the Semantic Scholar corpus, so raw coverage is similar and both inherit the same weakness: strongest in medicine, psychology, and the empirical sciences; thinner in the humanities and for very recent preprints.

The difference is in retrieval philosophy. Consensus optimizes for *answering the question you asked* — its top results are usually tightly on-topic. Elicit optimizes for *mapping the space* — it surfaces adjacent studies you didn't know to ask about, which is more useful when you're scoping a review and less useful when you want a fast answer. In our testing, neither hallucinated citations, which remains the key advantage of both over asking ChatGPT for references.

## Depth of analysis

This is where they diverge most.

Elicit's extraction tables are the deeper tool. Pulling sample sizes, effect directions, and methods across 30 papers into one table is hours of manual work that Elicit does in minutes — imperfectly, but with each cell linked to the source passage so verification is fast. Accuracy on clearly reported fields is good; on fields that require inference (a study's implicit limitations, say) it's hit and miss, and you must check.

Consensus is intentionally shallower per paper but faster to insight. The Consensus Meter is a genuinely honest summary device — it counts studies, not truth, and the interface says so — but it's easy to misread if you ignore study quality. Twenty small observational studies saying "yes" and two large RCTs saying "no" produce a misleading meter. The quality labels help; you still have to use them.

## Workflow fit

- **Writing a literature review or thesis chapter:** Elicit, clearly. The table workflow, PDF uploads, and CSV/BIB export slot straight into a systematic process. Pair it with a summarization step for the papers you shortlist — our guide to [using AI for research](/how-to-use-ai-for-research/) covers that pipeline.
- **Checking evidence before making a claim:** Consensus. Thirty seconds to a cited, balanced answer beats building a table you don't need.
- **Reading a specific paper you already have:** Neither is ideal alone — Elicit can extract from an uploaded PDF, but for interactive Q&A over one document, a dedicated PDF tool is better (see our [ChatPDF review](/reviews/chatpdf-review-2023/)).
- **Non-academic research** (market sizing, news, general web): neither — they only see scholarly literature.

## Pricing value

At roughly the same price point (~$9–12/month as of mid-2024), the value question is really a usage question. Consensus's free tier is generous enough that casual users may never pay: unlimited searches, with a monthly cap on the AI-powered analyses. Elicit's free credits are one-time, so regular use pushes you to a subscription faster. If you'd use either heavily, the cost is trivial next to the hours saved; if you're an occasional user, Consensus free covers more ground.

## Tips for getting more out of either tool

A few habits that improved our results in both:

- **Ask questions the literature can answer.** "Does intermittent fasting reduce inflammation markers in adults?" beats "is fasting good?" Both tools reward the specificity of a well-formed research question.
- **Run the same question twice with different phrasing.** Retrieval is sensitive to wording; two phrasings often surface meaningfully different paper sets, especially in Elicit.
- **Check the dates.** Both tools happily return decade-old studies alongside current ones. Filter or sort by year before drawing conclusions in fast-moving fields.
- **Follow the citation trail manually.** When a key paper appears, its references and the papers citing it are still best explored in Semantic Scholar or Google Scholar directly — neither tool replaces snowballing yet.

## Which should you choose?

- **Choose Elicit if** you're doing structured research — literature reviews, systematic reviews, evidence synthesis — where the deliverable is a table, a bibliography, or a chapter. It's the stronger *workbench*.
- **Choose Consensus if** you mostly need trustworthy, cited answers to discrete questions — a clinician sanity-checking a claim, a writer fact-checking, a student orienting in a new topic. It's the stronger *answer engine*.
- **Choose both if** research is your job. They compose well: scope the question in Consensus, then move to Elicit to do the systematic work. The combined cost is still under most single writing-tool subscriptions.

The honest summary: these tools compete for attention, not for the same task. Pick the one shaped like your actual workflow, and keep your skepticism — both put citations one click away precisely because you're still supposed to click.
