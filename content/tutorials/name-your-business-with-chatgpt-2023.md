Naming a business is one of those tasks that feels like it should take an afternoon and ends up taking three weeks. You write a list, hate everything on it, check a domain, find it's taken, and start over.

ChatGPT won't pick the perfect name for you, but it is very good at the part people get stuck on: producing lots of options in lots of styles, fast. Used well, it turns naming from staring at a blank page into editing a long list. Used badly, it hands you the same generic "-ify" and "-ly" names everyone else is getting.

This tutorial walks through a workflow that gets you to the good version. If you're new to the tool, start with our [getting started with ChatGPT guide](/getting-started-with-chatgpt-2023/) first.

## Step 1: Write a naming brief before you prompt

The biggest mistake is typing "give me names for a bakery." You'll get Golden Crust, Rise & Shine, and The Daily Loaf, which are names that already exist in a hundred towns.

Spend ten minutes writing a short brief. Cover:

- **What you do**, in one sentence
- **Who you serve**: local families, enterprise IT teams, dog owners in apartments
- **How you want to feel**: premium, playful, trustworthy, rebellious
- **What to avoid**: words you dislike, competitors' naming patterns, anything hard to spell
- **Practical constraints**: must work as a .com, must be under 12 letters, must make sense internationally

That brief is the context ChatGPT needs to stop defaulting to the middle of the road.

## Step 2: Generate in distinct styles, not one big list

Paste your brief and ask for names grouped by naming style. Different styles produce very different results:

```
Here is my business brief: [paste brief]

Generate 10 name ideas in each of these styles:
1. Descriptive (says what we do)
2. Evocative (a real word that suggests a feeling or benefit)
3. Invented (a new, pronounceable word)
4. Compound (two real words joined)
5. Founder or place-based

For each name, add a one-line rationale.
```

The rationales matter. They show you *why* a name was suggested, which helps you steer the next round, and they often surface a good idea hiding behind a weak name.

## Step 3: Iterate on what you like

Read the list and mark anything that gives you even a small spark. Then feed those back:

```
I like "Harborline" and "Fieldnote" best. What I like: they feel calm,
grounded, and slightly nautical/outdoorsy. Generate 20 more names in
that direction. Avoid anything ending in -ly, -ify, or -hub.
```

Telling ChatGPT what you *dislike* is as useful as telling it what you like. Explicit exclusions are the fastest way out of the generic zone.

Run three or four rounds of this. Each round should narrow the style while keeping variety in the actual words.

## Step 4: Use ChatGPT as a critic, not just a generator

Once you have a shortlist of 10-15, switch roles. Ask ChatGPT to poke holes:

```
Here is my shortlist: [names]. For each one, tell me:
- How it might be mispronounced or misspelled when heard aloud
- Any unfortunate meanings, slang, or associations in English
- Whether it sounds like a specific well-known brand
- How well it would age if we expand beyond [current product]
```

This catches problems you're too close to see, like a name that sounds fine written down but gets misheard over the phone. It won't catch everything, and its knowledge of other languages and slang is uneven, but it's a solid first filter.

## Step 5: Check availability yourself

This is where ChatGPT stops being useful. The free research preview has no internet access and a knowledge cutoff, so it cannot tell you whether a domain is available, whether a trademark exists, or whether a company down the street already uses the name. It may confidently say a name is "unique." Don't believe it.

For each shortlisted name, check:

1. **Domain**: search a registrar for the .com and any extensions you'd accept.
2. **Trademarks**: search your national trademark database (TESS in the US) in your product category.
3. **Business registry**: your state or country's business name search.
4. **Social handles**: the platforms you'll actually use.
5. **A plain Google search**: the fastest way to spot a direct competitor.

Expect to lose half your shortlist here. That's normal, and it's why Step 2 generates so many names.

## Step 6: Test with real people

Take your final three to five names to people in your target audience. Don't ask "which do you like?" Ask things that reveal how the name works:

- Say it out loud and ask them to spell it.
- Ask what they'd guess the business does.
- Ask which one they'd remember tomorrow.

You can use ChatGPT to draft a short survey or a set of questions for these conversations, but the answers need to come from humans.

## Tips for better results

**Give examples of names you admire.** "I like the tone of names like Patagonia, Mailchimp, and Stripe" communicates more than paragraphs of adjectives.

**Ask for constraints explicitly.** "Maximum two syllables" or "must start with a hard consonant" produces sharper lists than hoping it figures that out.

**Start new chats for fresh directions.** Long conversations drift toward earlier ideas. When a thread feels stuck, open a new chat, paste the brief, and try a different style.

**Save everything.** Copy promising names into a separate document as you go. It's easy to lose a good idea twenty messages up.

**Try taglines alongside names.** Asking for a tagline with each name shows you whether a name can carry a message or needs heavy explaining.

## Common pitfalls

- **Accepting the first batch.** The first ten results are the most predictable ones. The good stuff tends to show up on round three or four.
- **Trusting availability claims.** ChatGPT can't check anything live. Verify every name yourself.
- **Invented words that are hard to say.** A made-up name is easier to trademark, but if people can't spell it after hearing it, it'll cost you word-of-mouth.
- **Names too tied to one product.** "Best Cupcake Co." is awkward the day you start selling bread. Ask ChatGPT to flag names that limit future growth.
- **Forgetting the legal step.** For anything beyond a hobby, a quick consultation with a trademark attorney before you print signage is cheap insurance.

## Is ChatGPT the right tool for this?

For brainstorming, yes. It's faster than a thesaurus and more varied than a name generator website. Other chatbots are catching up; our [ChatGPT vs YouChat comparison](/compare/chatgpt-vs-youchat-2023/) covers how the alternatives stack up. But for the creative-volume part of naming, ChatGPT is the best free option available right now.

Where it falls short is anything requiring current, verified information. Keep that division clear: the AI generates and critiques, and you verify and decide. A good name comes out of a good process, and ChatGPT makes that process much faster.
