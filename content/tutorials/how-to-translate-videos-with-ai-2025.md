Video is the format that travels — but only if your audience can understand it. A clip that lands with an English-speaking audience is invisible to the billions who don't speak English. Until recently, translating a video meant hiring translators and voice actors or slapping on subtitles and hoping for the best. In 2025, AI has collapsed that into a workflow you can run yourself in an afternoon.

This guide walks through how to translate and dub a video with AI end to end — subtitles, voiceover, and lip-sync — plus the pitfalls that separate a professional result from an obviously robotic one.

## What "AI video translation" actually includes

There are three distinct things people mean by "translate my video," and good tools handle all three:

1. **Subtitles** — translated captions burned in or added as a track.
2. **Dubbing** — a new spoken audio track in the target language, replacing the original voice.
3. **Lip-sync** — adjusting the speaker's mouth movements so the dub looks natural, not overdubbed.

You don't always need all three. A talking-head explainer benefits from full dubbing and lip-sync; a b-roll-heavy montage might only need translated subtitles.

## Step 1: Start with clean source audio

AI translation is only as good as its transcription, and transcription is only as good as your audio. Before you translate anything:

- Use the cleanest version of your video you have — minimal background noise, one speaker at a time.
- If the audio is rough, run it through a cleanup tool first. Editors like [Descript](/reviews/04-descript-review/) or [CapCut](/reviews/capcut-ai-review-2024/) have one-tap noise reduction.
- Avoid heavy music under dialogue in the section you're translating; it confuses the transcriber.

Garbage in, garbage out applies hard here. Ten minutes cleaning audio saves an hour fixing mistranslations.

## Step 2: Pick the right tool for the job

Different tools specialize in different parts of the pipeline:

- **HeyGen** — strong for full dubbing *with lip-sync*, so a translated talking-head video looks like the person actually spoke the new language. See our [HeyGen review](/heygen-review-ai-avatar-videos/) for the avatar side of the product.
- **ElevenLabs** — excellent for the *voice* layer, with multilingual dubbing and voice cloning so the dubbed track can sound like the original speaker. Details in the [ElevenLabs review](/reviews/05-elevenlabs-review/).
- **CapCut** — good for translated *subtitles* and basic TTS, free, and fine for social clips.
- **Dedicated dubbing tools (Rask and similar)** — built specifically for multi-language video localization at volume.

Pick based on your priority: publish-ready subtitles fast (CapCut), natural voice (ElevenLabs), or realistic on-camera dubbing (HeyGen). Many creators chain two — clone the voice in one, sync the video in another.

## Step 3: Transcribe and translate the text first

Before you generate any audio, get the *text* right. Most tools do transcription and translation in one step, but always export the translated script and read it:

- Check names, brand terms, and technical jargon — AI often "translates" things that should stay untranslated.
- Watch for idioms that translate literally into nonsense.
- Adjust length. Some languages run 20–30% longer than English, which throws off timing and dubbing pace.

This review pass is the single highest-leverage step. Fixing text is fast; fixing a finished dub is not.

## Step 4: Generate the dub (and clone the voice if you can)

With the script approved, generate the audio in the target language. Two choices matter:

- **Voice cloning vs. stock voice.** A cloned voice keeps *your* sound across languages, which is far less jarring for your audience. A stock synthetic voice is faster but more generic.
- **Pacing.** Match the dub timing to the original clip. If the translation is longer, either speed the delivery slightly or edit the video to give it room. Tools with automatic timing help, but check the result.

If you're new to cloning, our [voice cloning guide](/tutorials/10-create-ai-voiceovers/) covers the setup and the ethics of using someone's voice.

## Step 5: Add lip-sync (if it's a talking-head video)

For on-camera speakers, lip-sync is what turns "obviously dubbed" into "looks native." Tools like HeyGen reshape the mouth movements to match the new audio. It's not perfect — extreme angles and fast speech still trip it up — but for standard framing it's convincing. Skip this step for voiceover-only or b-roll content; it adds nothing there.

## Step 6: Review, caption, and export

Before publishing:

- **Have a fluent speaker check the final cut** if the content matters. AI handles grammar well but misses cultural nuance and tone.
- **Add translated subtitles anyway**, even on a dubbed video — many viewers watch muted, and captions boost accessibility and reach.
- **Export per platform.** Vertical for social, horizontal for YouTube; re-generate captions per aspect ratio if needed.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Trusting the first pass.** Always read the translated script before generating audio.
- **Ignoring timing drift.** Longer translations desync from the visuals — fix pacing, don't ship it.
- **Over-relying on lip-sync.** It's impressive but imperfect; don't use it as a crutch for a bad dub.
- **Skipping the human check.** For anything customer-facing, a five-minute review by a native speaker prevents embarrassing errors.
- **Forgetting rights.** Only clone voices you have permission to use, and check each tool's licensing before publishing commercially.

## Putting it together

A realistic 2025 workflow looks like: clean the audio → transcribe and translate in one tool → review and fix the script → clone the voice and generate the dub in [ElevenLabs](/reviews/05-elevenlabs-review/) → sync it to video in HeyGen → add subtitles and export. The whole thing takes an afternoon for a video that used to require a localization team.

The payoff is real: one video, five languages, five times the audience. AI won't make your translation flawless on its own — but paired with a quick human review, it makes multilingual video a practical channel instead of a budget line item.
