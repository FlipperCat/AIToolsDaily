Lesson planning eats evenings. Writing objectives, sequencing activities, and building three versions of the same worksheet for different reading levels takes hours even when you know the content cold. ChatGPT, which OpenAI released as a free preview at the end of November, is good at exactly this kind of structured first draft.

It is not a curriculum expert, and it will confidently get things wrong. Used as a drafting assistant, it can turn a 90-minute planning session into 30 minutes of editing. This guide walks through the workflow step by step, with prompts you can copy.

If you are new to the tool itself, start with our plain-English explainer on [what ChatGPT is and how it works](/what-is-chatgpt-explained-2023/).

## What ChatGPT Is Good (and Bad) At for Planning

**Good at:**
- Turning a topic and grade level into a sensible lesson structure
- Writing learning objectives in consistent, measurable language
- Generating warm-ups, discussion questions, and exit tickets
- Rewriting a passage or task at different reading levels
- Brainstorming hooks and real-world connections

**Bad at:**
- Facts after 2021. Its training data stops there, and it has no internet access.
- Citing sources. It will make up book titles, studies, and URLs that look real.
- Knowing your students, your pacing guide, or your school's materials
- Math and science details. It can make arithmetic mistakes and state them with total confidence.

The rule that follows from this: ChatGPT drafts the structure, and you supply the facts and the judgment.

## Step 1: Gather Your Constraints Before You Type

The quality of the output depends almost entirely on how much context you give it. Before you open ChatGPT, jot down:

- Grade level and subject
- The specific standard or objective (copy the exact text)
- Class length (45 minutes, a 90-minute block)
- What students already know from previous lessons
- Materials you have: textbook chapter, lab equipment, devices or no devices
- Class makeup: English learners, IEP accommodations, a wide reading range

Two minutes spent on this list saves several rounds of "no, make it shorter" later.

## Step 2: Write a Context-Rich Prompt

Here is a template that works well. Replace the bracketed parts:

```
You are an experienced [grade] [subject] teacher.
Write a lesson plan for a [length]-minute class on [topic].

Standard: [paste exact standard text]
Students already know: [prior knowledge]
Materials available: [list]
Class notes: [e.g., 6 English learners, two students with
extended-time accommodations, no student devices]

Include: a measurable learning objective, a 5-minute warm-up,
direct instruction, a guided practice activity, independent
practice, and a 3-question exit ticket. Give approximate
minutes for each section.
```

Asking for minutes per section matters. Without it, ChatGPT tends to produce plans that would take two class periods.

## Step 3: Ask for an Outline First, Then Expand

For a new unit or an unfamiliar topic, don't ask for the full plan in one go. Ask for a one-paragraph outline of the lesson flow first. Read it, fix the sequence, and then ask it to expand:

```
Good. Keep that structure but swap the guided practice for a
think-pair-share. Now write out the full plan, including the
exact questions I should ask during direct instruction.
```

ChatGPT remembers the earlier messages in the same conversation, so you can refine in small steps instead of rewriting the whole prompt. Keep one conversation per unit so the context builds up, and rename it in the sidebar so you can find it next week.

## Step 4: Generate Differentiation in One Pass

This is where it saves the most time. Once you have a core activity, ask for versions:

```
Rewrite the independent practice task in three versions:
1. For students reading about two grade levels below
2. On grade level
3. An extension for students who finish early

Keep the same learning objective for all three.
```

For English learners, you can ask it to add sentence frames ("I think ___ because ___") or a short vocabulary list with student-friendly definitions. Always read the simplified versions carefully. It sometimes simplifies by dropping the hard part of the concept instead of scaffolding it.

## Step 5: Build Quick Assessments

ChatGPT writes decent formative checks. Some prompts to try:

- "Write five multiple-choice questions on this lesson. Make each wrong answer a common student misconception, and explain which misconception each one targets."
- "Write a 3-question exit ticket: one recall, one application, one that asks students to explain their reasoning."
- "Create a simple 4-point rubric for the paragraph response in this lesson."

The misconception prompt is worth trying. Wrong answers built on real errors tell you far more than random ones do.

**Check every answer key yourself.** This is non-negotiable in math and science, where it can mark a wrong answer as correct.

## Step 6: Fact-Check and Align

Before a plan goes anywhere near students, do a pass for:

1. **Accuracy.** Verify every fact, date, formula, and definition against your textbook or another trusted source.
2. **Invented sources.** If it suggests a book, article, or video, confirm it exists. Assume it doesn't until you find it.
3. **Standards.** Compare the objective and the exit ticket to the standard text you pasted in. Does the assessment actually measure the objective?
4. **Timing.** Read through it as if you were teaching it. Most AI drafts are too ambitious for real class periods with real transitions.
5. **Fit.** Swap generic examples for ones your students will recognize, like local places, school events, and things they care about.

## Step 7: Save Your Best Prompts

Once a prompt produces a plan you like, save it in a document. A personal prompt library is the real long-term payoff. Next time it's a copy, paste, and change the topic. Teachers who share prompts within a department get even more out of this, since one well-tuned template can serve a whole grade team.

## Tips That Make a Noticeable Difference

- **Give it a role.** "You are an experienced 7th grade science teacher" produces more grade-appropriate language than no role at all.
- **Set length limits.** "Keep the warm-up to three sentences" stops it from padding.
- **Ask for a table.** "Format the plan as a table with columns for time, activity, and teacher moves" gives you something printable.
- **Regenerate.** If a response is flat, the "Regenerate response" button often gives a noticeably different take.
- **Ask it to critique itself.** "What might confuse a struggling student in this plan?" surfaces gaps you can fix before class.

## Common Pitfalls

**Using the first draft as-is.** The output looks polished, which makes it tempting to print. Polished is not the same as correct or right for your class.

**Leaving out student context.** Without it, you get a plan for an imaginary average class that doesn't exist.

**Pasting in student information.** Don't include student names, grades, or IEP details. Describe needs in general terms ("three students need extended time") instead. Anything you type is sent to OpenAI, and conversations may be reviewed to improve the system.

**Expecting current events.** A lesson on something that happened last month will be missing information or simply wrong. Supply the facts yourself and let ChatGPT handle the structure.

**Letting it plan the whole unit blind.** ChatGPT is strong at single lessons and weak at long-range sequencing across weeks, where it loses track of what came before. Plan the unit arc yourself and use it lesson by lesson.

## The Bottom Line

ChatGPT won't replace your judgment about what your students need, and it shouldn't. What it does well is remove the blank-page problem: objectives, activity structures, differentiated versions, and quiz drafts in minutes instead of hours. Give it rich context, treat every output as a draft, and verify every fact.
