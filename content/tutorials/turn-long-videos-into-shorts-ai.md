Every hour-long podcast, webinar, or livestream contains a handful of moments that would work as standalone shorts. Finding them used to be the bottleneck: scrub the footage, note timestamps, crop to vertical, caption everything. AI clipping tools have collapsed that pipeline into about twenty minutes of work per episode. Here's the workflow I've settled on in 2025 after running several podcasts' worth of footage through the major tools — including where the AI needs adult supervision.

## The toolkit

You need three capabilities, sometimes in one tool:

1. **Clip detection** — AI that scans the transcript and flags self-contained, high-energy moments. Opus Clip and Vizard are the specialists here.
2. **Auto-reframing** — converting 16:9 to 9:16 while keeping the speaker (or the right speaker) in frame.
3. **Captions** — styled, word-by-word captions, since most short-form viewing starts muted.

Opus Clip does all three and is the default recommendation for podcasts. [Descript](/reviews/04-descript-review/) is the better pick if you also edit the long-form episode there, since clipping becomes part of the same project. CapCut is the free-ish manual fallback — more control, more clicks; see our [Descript vs CapCut comparison](/compare/descript-vs-capcut/) for how those philosophies differ.

## Step 1: Feed it the right source material

Garbage in, garbage out applies double here. Before uploading:

- **Use footage with clear speech and minimal crosstalk.** Clip detection runs on the transcript. If the transcript is mush, the clip scores are mush.
- **Interviews and solo commentary outperform panels.** Two speakers reframe cleanly; four people talking over each other confuse both the AI and the viewer.
- **Upload the highest resolution you have.** A 9:16 crop from 1080p footage uses roughly a third of the frame width — starting from 4K keeps the final short crisp.

Paste a YouTube link or upload the file directly. For a one-hour episode, processing typically takes several minutes.

## Step 2: Triage the AI's clip suggestions

Opus Clip returns a ranked list of candidate clips, each with a "virality" score. Treat the score as a rough sorting mechanism, not a verdict — it rewards hooks, questions, and emotional language, and it has no idea what your audience actually cares about.

My triage rules:

- **Watch the first 3 seconds of each candidate.** No hook in 3 seconds, no clip. Skip it regardless of score.
- **Check that the clip is self-contained.** The most common AI failure is a clip that references "what we just talked about." If it needs context, kill it or re-trim the start.
- **Aim to keep 30-40%.** From ten suggestions, three or four survive. That's normal and fine.
- **Re-trim boundaries manually.** The AI errs toward starting a beat early and ending a beat late. Tightening both ends by a second or two noticeably improves the clip.

This is the step people skip, and it's why so many repurposed-content feeds feel like sludge. The AI finds *candidates*; you're still the editor.

## Step 3: Fix the reframing

Auto-reframing tracks the active speaker and crops to them. It works well until it doesn't:

- **Two-person shots:** check every cut point. Trackers occasionally linger on the listener while the speaker talks. Most tools let you override the crop per scene — do it.
- **Screen shares and slides:** auto-crop mangles these. Switch to a split layout (speaker on bottom, screen on top) or drop the segment.
- **B-roll moments:** if the speaker gestures at something off-frame, the vertical crop loses it. Either widen to a letterboxed layout for that beat or cut around it.

Thirty seconds of checking per clip saves you from the classic repurposing tell: a vertical video awkwardly cropped on the wrong person.

## Step 4: Captions and styling

Word-by-word animated captions are non-negotiable for muted autoplay feeds. Every major tool generates them; your job is restraint:

1. Pick one caption style and use it on every clip — consistency reads as a brand.
2. Proofread names, brands, and jargon. Transcription still fumbles proper nouns, and a misspelled guest name is an embarrassing way to lose that guest's reshare.
3. Keep captions in the lower-middle safe zone, clear of each platform's UI overlays.
4. Skip the emoji-every-word preset. It tested worse for me on everything except explicitly casual TikTok content.

Add a small logo or handle watermark — clips get ripped, and the watermark is your attribution insurance.

## Step 5: Publish deliberately, not all at once

Ten clips dumped in one day perform worse than the same clips spread across two weeks. A cadence that works:

- **Lead with your second-best clip** on day one (save the best for mid-run, when the algorithm has data on the series).
- **One clip per day, per platform,** natively uploaded — cross-posted watermarked videos get suppressed on some platforms.
- **Rewrite the hook text per platform.** The same clip earns different captions on TikTok, Reels, and Shorts.
- **Route winners back to the source.** Pin a comment linking the full episode; that's the entire point of repurposing.

Most clipping tools now include schedulers, which are serviceable. If you're already paying for a social scheduler, keep using it and just export the files.

## What this costs

Approximate pricing as of May 2025: Opus Clip's paid plans start around $15/month for enough processing hours to cover a weekly show; Vizard is in a similar band; Descript starts around $12-24/month depending on tier and covers your whole editing workflow, not just clipping. Free tiers exist across the board but watermark exports or cap monthly minutes — fine for testing, not for shipping. Prices shift often, so check current pages.

## The bottom line

AI clipping tools solved the tedious 80% of repurposing: finding candidate moments, reframing, and captioning. They did not solve taste. The creators winning with this workflow spend their saved hours on selection and hooks — the parts the AI is worst at. Run the pipeline, keep a third of what it suggests, and publish on a schedule.
