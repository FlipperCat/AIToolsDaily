Most small teams already use AI at work, whether or not anyone approved it. Someone drafts proposals in a chatbot, someone else summarizes client calls with a meeting assistant, and a contractor uses an image generator for social posts. That's usually fine. The problem is that nobody has agreed on what's allowed. Can you paste client data in? Does AI-written copy need a human check? Which accounts should people use?

An AI usage policy answers those questions in one short document. Here's how to write one that people will actually read and follow, in about an afternoon.

## Step 1: Find Out What People Already Use

Don't write rules in a vacuum. Send a short, no-blame survey:

- Which AI tools do you use for work, and for what?
- Are you on a free, personal, or company account?
- What kind of information do you put into them?

You'll likely find more tools than you expected, and a mix of personal and company accounts. That list becomes the starting point for your approved tools. The survey also tells you which uses matter most to the team, so the policy supports them instead of blocking them.

## Step 2: Classify Your Data (Keep It Simple)

The core of any AI policy is what information can go where. Three tiers are enough for most small businesses:

| Tier | Examples | AI rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Published blog posts, public website copy, general industry questions | Any approved tool |
| **Internal** | Internal docs, draft plans, non-sensitive meeting notes | Approved tools on company accounts with training on your data disabled |
| **Restricted** | Client personal data, financials, passwords, health info, contracts under NDA | Not allowed in AI tools, unless a specific tool has been vetted and approved for that use |

Put examples from your own business in each row. "Client personal data" is abstract. "The customer list export from our CRM" is clear.

If your team needs a refresher on why this matters, point them to our guide on [AI privacy and security](/ai-privacy-and-security-what-to-know/).

## Step 3: Approve a Short List of Tools

For each tool you approve, write down:

- **What it's approved for** (drafting, coding, transcription, images)
- **Which account type** (company workspace, not personal)
- **The highest data tier allowed**
- **Who owns the account** and handles adding and removing users

Prefer business or team plans. They typically give admin controls, the option to exclude your data from model training, and a way to remove access when someone leaves. Those controls are the main reason to pay for a team plan.

Keep the list short. Three to five tools is plenty for most teams. Each extra subscription adds cost and one more place for data to end up. Our guide to [saving money on AI subscriptions](/how-to-save-money-on-ai-tool-subscriptions/) can help you consolidate.

For requests to add a new tool, set a lightweight process: someone runs a time-boxed trial and checks the data terms before it's approved. Our walkthrough on [evaluating an AI tool's free trial](/tutorials/how-to-evaluate-an-ai-tool-during-a-free-trial-2026-a-practical-testing-checklist/) works well as that process.

## Step 4: Set Review Standards for AI Output

The data rules protect what goes *into* AI. The review rules cover what comes *out*. A simple principle works: **the person who ships it owns it.** Using AI doesn't move responsibility for accuracy to the tool.

Then set expectations by type of work:

- **Client-facing writing:** a human reads the whole piece before it's sent, and checks every fact, number, name, and link.
- **Code:** AI-generated code goes through the same review and testing as any other code. Nobody merges code they don't understand.
- **Research and summaries:** check key claims against the original source before acting on them. AI summaries can drop important caveats or invent details.
- **Images and media:** check for obvious errors, and don't generate images of real people, logos, or copyrighted characters for commercial use without approval.

## Step 5: Decide on Disclosure

Decide when you tell clients or the public that AI was involved. Businesses differ here, so choose deliberately:

- Some teams disclose whenever AI produced a large share of a deliverable.
- Some disclose only when a client contract requires it, or when AI is talking to customers directly, as with a support chatbot.
- Almost everyone should disclose when a customer is interacting with an AI rather than a person.

Check your client contracts too. Some agreements now include clauses about AI use, and your policy shouldn't contradict them.

## Step 6: Cover the Edge Cases

A few short lines prevent the most common problems:

- **Personal accounts:** not allowed for work involving internal or restricted data.
- **Browser extensions and plug-ins:** AI add-ons that read your screen or email need approval, because they can see far more than a chat box.
- **Meeting recorders:** tell attendees when a meeting is being recorded or transcribed, and follow the recording-consent rules where you operate.
- **Agents with account access:** AI tools that can send email, make purchases, or change files on your behalf need explicit approval and a limited scope.
- **Mistakes:** if someone pastes restricted data into a tool by accident, they report it right away with no penalty for self-reporting. Fast reporting matters more than blame.

## Step 7: Write It Short and Roll It Out

Aim for one to two pages. A long policy won't get read. A simple structure:

```
1. Purpose (two sentences: we use AI to work faster; these rules protect clients and quality)
2. Approved tools (table: tool, use, account type, max data tier, owner)
3. Data rules (the three tiers with our examples)
4. Review rules (the person who ships it owns it + per-work-type checks)
5. Disclosure (when we tell clients/customers)
6. Edge cases (personal accounts, extensions, recorders, agents)
7. Mistakes and questions (who to tell, no-blame reporting)
8. Review date (every six months)
```

Walk through it in a 20-minute team meeting instead of just emailing it. Ask people to raise uses the policy doesn't cover. Their questions will show you what's missing.

## Common Pitfalls

**Being too restrictive.** If the rules make the approved path harder than the shadow path, people will take the shadow path. Make the approved tools easy to use.

**Naming tools without owners.** Every approved tool needs a person responsible for its account and access.

**Writing it once and forgetting it.** Vendors change data terms and features often. Put the six-month review on the calendar.

**Treating it as a legal document.** It's an operating guide. If you're in a regulated industry, have an expert review it, but write it in plain language for the team first.

## The Bottom Line

A good AI policy doesn't slow your team down. It lets people use AI confidently, because they know what's allowed. Start with a quick survey, a three-tier data table, a short list of approved tools, and one rule: the person who ships it owns it. You can have a working version by the end of the week and improve it from there.
