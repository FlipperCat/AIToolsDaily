Deep Research is the first ChatGPT feature that produces something you could plausibly hand to your boss. Give it a research question, wait five to thirty minutes while an agent browses dozens of sources, and you get back a long, structured, cited report — closer to an analyst's first draft than a chatbot answer.

It's also easy to use badly. With Plus giving you only a handful of queries per month, a lazy one-line prompt wastes a scarce resource on a mediocre report. This guide covers how to consistently get reports worth the wait.

## What Deep Research actually does

Unlike a normal ChatGPT response (seconds, trained knowledge plus maybe a quick search), Deep Research runs an autonomous browsing agent powered by one of OpenAI's reasoning models. It plans a research strategy, searches, opens and reads pages, follows leads, revises its plan when it hits dead ends, and then synthesizes everything into a report with inline citations. You can watch its progress — searches and sources — in a sidebar while it works.

It's available on Pro and, as of late February, on Plus and Team plans. Google shipped a similar feature first — our [Gemini Deep Research review](/reviews/gemini-deep-research-review-2025/) covers it, and we've compared the two head-to-head [here](/compare/chatgpt-deep-research-vs-gemini-deep-research-2025/).

## Step 1: Pick questions that deserve it

Deep Research shines on questions that are *broad, synthetic, and source-heavy* — the kind that would take you an afternoon of tabs:

- "Compare the leading email marketing platforms for a 10-person e-commerce team, with pricing and migration effort"
- "What does published research say about four-day workweek trials — outcomes, methodologies, criticisms?"
- "Map the competitive landscape for AI meeting transcription and identify underserved niches"

It's the *wrong* tool for single facts, anything you need in under a minute, questions answerable from one obvious source, and topics where most of the good material sits behind paywalls it can't cross. Save your monthly queries for genuine synthesis work; regular chat with search handles the rest, as we covered in our broader guide to [using AI for research](/how-to-use-ai-for-research/).

## Step 2: Write a real brief, not a question

The single biggest quality lever. A Deep Research prompt should read like instructions to a junior analyst:

**Context.** Who's asking and why. "I run marketing for a B2B SaaS company with a $3k/month tools budget" changes every downstream decision the agent makes.

**Scope.** What's in and what's out. Time ranges ("developments since 2023"), geographies, market segments, and explicit exclusions ("ignore enterprise-only tools").

**Format.** Ask for the structure you want: an executive summary, a comparison table, sections per competitor, a recommendations section. You'll get dramatically more usable output than the default essay wall.

**Source preferences.** "Prioritize primary sources, official documentation, and published studies; treat vendor marketing pages as claims, not facts" measurably improves citation quality.

A useful trick: draft the brief in a regular chat first. Paste your rough question and ask, "Turn this into a detailed research brief — ask me anything that's ambiguous." Refining is free there; Deep Research queries aren't.

## Step 3: Take the clarifying questions seriously

Before starting, Deep Research usually asks two to four clarifying questions — scope, priorities, output format. Don't wave them off with "whatever you think is best." Every question it asks marks an ambiguity it will otherwise resolve by guessing, and a wrong guess at this stage skews the entire twenty-minute run. Two sentences per answer is plenty.

## Step 4: Use the wait productively

Queue queries before meetings, lunch, or deep-work blocks — you'll be notified when the report lands. Glancing at the sidebar mid-run is occasionally worthwhile: if you see it chasing an irrelevant tangent, that's feedback your brief was underspecified. You can't steer mid-run, but you'll write a better follow-up.

## Step 5: Verify before you reuse

The report reads authoritative. Resist taking it at face value:

1. **Spot-check load-bearing claims.** Any number, quote, or conclusion you plan to repeat — open the citation and confirm the source actually says it. Misrepresented sources are rarer than old-school hallucinations but they absolutely happen.
2. **Audit source quality.** Skim the citation list. SEO content farms and thin listicles sneak in; if a key section rests on weak sources, treat that section as a lead to verify, not a finding.
3. **Check recency.** The agent can't read paywalled journals or anything behind a login, so it occasionally leans on older public material when newer gated work exists. For fast-moving topics, note the publication dates it's citing.

## Step 6: Iterate in the same thread

The report isn't the end of the conversation. Follow-ups in the same thread — "expand the pricing section," "turn this into a one-page summary for a non-technical exec," "what's the strongest counterargument to this recommendation?" — use the completed research as context and cost you nothing. Extract maximum value from each expensive run with cheap follow-ups.

## Budgeting your queries

With roughly 10 queries a month on Plus, treat them like a resource: one for a client or work deliverable, one or two for a big personal decision, and keep a couple in reserve for whatever the month throws at you. If you're consistently maxing out, that's the actual argument for Pro — or for offloading lighter recurring research to a tool like Perplexity, which we've covered in a [separate workflow guide](/tutorials/automate-research-with-perplexity/).

## The bottom line

Deep Research doesn't replace your judgment; it replaces the afternoon of tab-juggling that precedes judgment. The formula that works: spend five minutes on a proper brief, answer the clarifying questions like they matter, verify the citations behind anything you'll repeat, and mine each report with free follow-ups. Do that, and a handful of monthly queries covers a surprising share of serious research work.
